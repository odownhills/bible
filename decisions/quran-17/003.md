## 17:57 to see which of them is nearest | even the nearest of them
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Arabic has only the words "which of them is nearest" here, set straight after "they seek the means of approach to their Lord" with nothing to join them to it. The Quran uses the very same words as a question that hangs on the verb before it: "your fathers and your sons, you do not know which of them is nearest to you in benefit" (4:11). And it hangs such a question on a verb of doing, with nothing in between, where people are settling who it will be: "when they cast their pens, which of them would take charge of Mary" (3:44); so also "to test them, which of them is best in deeds" (18:7; so 67:2). Read that way, the ones who are called on are each seeking to be the one nearest to their Lord. The Quran speaks of nearness to God as a thing that is raced for: "And the ones who are first, the first: those are the ones brought near" (56:10-11); "and for that let those who compete compete" (83:26). And it says of prophets what this verse goes on to say of these: "they used to hurry to do good, and call on us in longing and in dread" (21:90).
other: The same word can also mean "whichever", and the Quran uses it so: "Then we will pull out of every party whichever of them was strongest in rebellion against the All-Merciful" (19:69). Read that way, the words say which of them are doing the seeking: the very ones who are nearest to God, and so the rest all the more. The Quran calls some of those who are worshiped "brought near", and says this of them: "The Messiah will never think it beneath him to be a servant of God, nor will the angels who are brought near" (4:172); Jesus is "one of those brought near" (3:45); "Those who are with your Lord are not too arrogant to worship him" (7:206); and of those who are said to be God's children, "Rather, they are honored servants ... and they tremble for fear of him" (21:26-28).
review: open

## 17:62 put a bridle on his descendants | devour his descendants
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Arabic verb is made from the word for the jaw. Said of a horse, it is to put a rope in its mouth so that it can be led. The answer Iblis is given speaks of being followed and of mastery, and of horses: "Whoever of them follows you" (17:63); "unsettle whomever of them you can with your voice, and rally your horses and your men on foot against them" (17:64); "As for my servants, you have no authority over them" (17:65). Wherever else the Quran gives this vow of his, it is a vow to lead people, with the same few left out: "I will certainly lead them all into the wrong course, except your chosen servants among them" (15:39-40; 38:82-83); "I will certainly lead them astray ... and I will certainly command them" (4:119). The Quran says how it turned out: "Iblis proved true what he thought of them, so they followed him, except a group of the believers" (34:20). And Satan himself says at the end that this was all his power: "I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you answered me" (14:22).
other: The same verb is said of locusts that eat a land bare, and so it can mean to devour a thing or wipe it out. What Iblis leads people to is their ruin, and the answers he is given say so: "Hell is the repayment of you all" (17:63); "Whoever of them follows you, I will certainly fill Hell with you all" (7:18; so 38:85). So also "whoever takes Satan as an ally apart from God has suffered a clear loss" (4:119), and of Satan and the man who listens to him, "the end of both is that they are in the Fire" (59:16-17).
review: open
