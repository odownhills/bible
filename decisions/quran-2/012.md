## 2:265 and to make themselves firm | and out of a firmness within themselves
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Arabic is "a making firm from themselves", set beside "seeking to please God", and the question is whether it is a second thing they aim at or the place their giving comes from. The Quran has this noun in one other place, and there doing what one is told is what makes a person firm: "if they had done what they were counseled to do, it would have been better for them and stronger in making them firm" (4:66). Being made firm is something asked for and given: "make our feet firm" (2:250); "God makes those who believe firm with the firm word" (14:27; so also 16:102). And the picture that follows is of a garden that gives back more for what falls on it.
other: "From themselves" is how the Quran says where a thing comes from: "a messenger from among yourselves" (9:128), "he created spouses for you from yourselves" (30:21), "out of envy from their own selves" (2:109). Read that way their giving comes out of something firm within them, and that sets them against the man just described, who "spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in God and the Last Day" (2:264): his giving is for other people's eyes, and theirs comes from within.
review: open

## 2:275 interest | usury
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Arabic word means growth, and it names what a debt grows by. This passage says how much of it has to go: "give up what remains of interest" (2:278), and "if you repent, you have your capital" (2:279). So everything over the sum that was lent is meant, whether it is small or large. The Quran plays on the word's meaning in the same way elsewhere: "what you give in interest so that it may grow in people's wealth does not grow with God" (30:39). And it is forbidden with no measure named: "their taking interest, though they were forbidden it" (4:161).
other: "Usury" is a charge so heavy that it ruins the one who owes, and the Quran does speak of this thing as multiplying: "do not eat up interest, doubled and multiplied" (3:130). This passage sets it against charity (2:276), calls the taking of it a wrong, "you do not wrong, and you are not wronged" (2:279), and turns at once to a debtor "in difficulty" who is to be given time (2:280). Read that way, what is forbidden is the growth that preys on a debtor.
review: open

## 2:282 And let no scribe or witness be harmed | And let no scribe or witness do harm
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Arabic verb form can be read either way, as "be harmed" or as "do harm". The verse goes straight on, "And if you do, it is defiance on your part", and all through this verse "you" are the people making the debt, who are told to write it down and to call witnesses, while the scribe and the witness are spoken of as "he" and "they". So the ones who could do this are the people making the debt, and the ones it would be done to are the scribe and the witness. The verse has already laid duties on those two, that they must not refuse, and here it shields them, as the same verb shields both parents: "Let no mother be harmed through her child, nor one to whom a child is born through his child" (2:233). The Quran uses this verb for harm done to someone who can be pressed: "do not keep them to harm them" (2:231); "do not harm them so as to make things tight for them" (65:6).
other: The other commands about the scribe and the witness in this verse have the same shape, and they say what those two must not do: "let no scribe refuse to write", "let the witnesses not refuse when they are called". Read that way this one goes on in the same line. The scribe is not to do harm by what he writes, when he has been told to write "justly" and "as God has taught him", nor the witness by what he says or keeps back: "do not conceal testimony. And whoever conceals it, his heart is sinful" (2:283). At 2:233 too the same form can be read as "let no mother do harm".
review: open
