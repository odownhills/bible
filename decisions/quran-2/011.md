## 2:255 the Ever-Standing | the Upholder of all
kind: both meant
call: ask
why: The Arabic word is a strong form of the word for standing. Plain standing is what that word means of people and of things: Zechariah "was standing, praying" (3:39), and of the towns that were destroyed, "some of them are standing and some are mown down" (11:100). The verse goes straight on to this: "Neither drowsiness nor sleep takes hold of him." The word stands beside "the Living", and the Quran sets sleep beside death: "God takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died, in their sleep" (39:42). So he is "the Living who does not die" (25:58) and the one who is always standing. Before him it is others who go down: "faces are humbled before the Living, the Ever-Standing" (20:111).
other: With a small word after it, the same word is standing over a thing and keeping it up: "Is he who stands over every soul with what it has earned ...?" (13:33), and God bears witness "upholding justice" (3:18). Everything else stands by him: "among his signs is that the sky and the earth stand by his command" (30:25); "God holds the heavens and the earth so that they do not pass away" (35:41). And this verse ends there too: "His seat takes in the heavens and the earth, and watching over them does not tire him."
review: open

## 2:256 in false gods | in the one who goes beyond all bounds
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: Elsewhere the Quran uses this word of what people worship in place of God: "Worship God and shun false gods" (16:36); "those who shun false gods and do not worship them, and turn to God" (39:17); "and worshiped false gods" (5:60). Here it is set against believing in God, and the next verse speaks of them as many: "their allies are false gods; they bring them out of the light into the darkness" (2:257). And the Quran says of them, "what they call on apart from him is falsehood" (22:62; 31:30).
other: The word is formed from the verb for going beyond all bounds, which is said of Pharaoh, "Go to Pharaoh; he has gone beyond all bounds" (20:24), and whose noun is "excess" at 2:15. So it can name a single one who does that. It is one at 4:60, with the same words "disbelieve in" as here: "they want to take their disputes to him, though they were commanded to disbelieve in him." And where the two sides are set against each other as they are in the next verse, this one stands beside Satan: "those who disbelieve fight in his way, so fight the allies of Satan" (4:76). Satan is called the ally of such people: "so he is their ally today" (16:63; so also 7:27).
review: open

## 2:259 how we lift them up | how we bring them to life
kind: readings differ
call: clear
why: The main text always follows the Hafs reading, and its verb is to make a thing rise from where it is: "when you are told, 'Rise,' then rise" (58:11). It is said of the bones before the flesh is put on them, and the Quran tells of the making of a human being in that same order, the bones first and then the flesh over them: "then we created the lump into bones, then we clothed the bones with flesh" (23:14).
other: Another established reading of the same letters, with one dot left off, makes the verb "bring to life", the word of "then, when he wills, he brings him to life" (80:22; so also 21:21). That answers what the man asked, "How will God give life to this after its death?" The same words stand at 35:9, with this word straight after them: "we gave life with it to the earth after its death. That is how the bringing to life will be." And of bones the Quran says, "Who will give life to bones when they have crumbled? Say: The one who produced them the first time will give them life" (36:78-79).
review: open

## 2:259 he said, "I know that God has power over all things." | he said, "Know that God has power over all things."
kind: readings differ
call: clear
why: The main text always follows the Hafs reading, in which the man speaks. The words follow "when it became clear to him", so the one who now sees is the one who says what he sees, and he is the one who had asked, "How will God give life to this after its death?" The Quran tells of others in the same shape, who are shown a thing and then speak for themselves: Moses, "when he recovered, he said, 'Glory to you! I repent to you, and I am the first of the believers'" (7:143); and the queen, "My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I have submitted with Solomon to God, the Lord of the worlds" (27:44).
other: Another established reading of the same letters makes it a command, "Know", said to the man by the one who has been speaking to him. That one has been giving him commands all along: "look at your food", "look at your donkey", "look at the bones". The next story ends with the same command, "And know that God is mighty, wise" (2:260), and this surah often closes a matter with it (2:194, 196, 203, 223, 231, 233, 235, 244).
review: open

## 2:260 take four birds and draw them to you | take four birds to you and cut them up
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Arabic verb comes only here in the Quran. Its ordinary sense is to turn or bend a thing toward oneself, and "to you" stands straight after it, which goes with drawing and not with cutting. What follows fits birds that have come to know him: "then call them; they will come to you in haste." And "a part of them" need not be pieces. The one other place where the Quran says "for each" and "a part of them" is of a crowd shared out among the seven gates of Hell: "for each gate there is a part of them assigned" (15:44).
other: The same verb was also used for cutting a thing up, and then "to you" goes with "take". Abraham asked to be shown "how you give life to the dead", and birds that are alive when they are called show nothing of that. The story just before is of a dead man raised up, and of bones lifted and clothed with flesh (2:259). And "place a part of them on each mountain" reads most simply of pieces.
review: open
