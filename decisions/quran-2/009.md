## 2:204 whose speech impresses you in the life of this world | whose speech about the life of this world impresses you
kind: grammar
call: clear
why: The question is what "in the life of this world" goes with: the impressing, or the speech. Everywhere else the Quran has these words, they say when or where a thing happens, and they are often set against what comes after: "You have disputed on their behalf in the life of this world, but who will dispute with God on their behalf on the Day of Resurrection?" (4:109); "God makes those who believe firm with the firm word in the life of this world and in the hereafter" (14:27; so also 2:85; 9:55; 10:64). Of people like this one it says, "when you see them, their bodies impress you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech" (63:4). And this passage goes straight on to what waits for him after this life: "So Hell is enough for him" (2:206).
other: The Arabic sets "in the life of this world" straight after "his speech", and after a word of speaking the same small word can mark what is spoken about: "do not speak to me about those who did wrong" (11:37; 23:27), "who disputes about God without knowledge" (22:3). The passage has just told of people whose whole prayer is "Our Lord, give to us in this world" (2:200), and it soon says, "The life of this world has been made attractive to those who disbelieve" (2:212). And the Quran shows how talk of this world's goods wins people: "those who wanted the life of this world said, 'If only we had the like of what Korah has been given'" (28:79).
review: open

## 2:205 And when he turns away | And when he takes charge
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: With nothing after it, this verb is "turn away" every other time it comes in this surah: "Then you turned away after that" (2:64; so also 2:83, 137, 246). The two verses set what he says to your face, "whose speech impresses you", against what he does once he has gone, as the Quran does with such people elsewhere: "They say, 'Obedience,' but when they go out from you, a group of them plan by night something other than what you say" (4:81; so also 2:14). And the same verb is used of a man who leaves and sets to work on his scheme: "So Pharaoh turned away and gathered his plot" (20:60).
other: The same verb can mean to take charge of a thing: "the one of them who took charge of the greater part of it" (24:11). It stands beside corruption on the earth once more, in a question that can itself be read either way, "if you turned away" or "if you took charge": "would you cause corruption on the earth and cut your ties of kinship?" (47:22). And destroying crops and offspring is what a man with power over a land can do: "Kings, when they enter a town, corrupt it" (27:34); Pharaoh "was high in the land", slaughtering its sons, and "he was one of those who cause corruption" (28:4).
review: open

## 2:208 enter into peace | enter into submission
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The Arabic word here is not the Quran's usual word for submission, which stands at 3:19 and 3:85. The Quran has this word in two other places, where the readers differ only over one vowel, and both times it is peace as against war: "And if they incline to peace, incline to it" (8:61); "So do not weaken and call for peace when you have the upper hand" (47:35). This verse sets it against an enemy, "He is a clear enemy to you", and Satan is called that where he sets people against one another: "Satan stirs up trouble among them. Satan is a clear enemy to man" (17:53; so also 12:5), and "Satan only wants to cast enmity and hatred among you" (5:91). When he made the first pair slip, what followed was "enemies to one another" (2:36). The man just described is "the fiercest in a quarrel" (2:204). And the next words, "if you slip after the clear proofs have come to you" (2:209), use the phrase the Quran keeps for people who fell out with one another: "no one differed over it except those who were given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, out of resentment among themselves" (2:213); "those after them would not have fought one another after the clear proofs had come to them" (2:253; so also 3:105). "All together" is said of people acting as one body (9:36, 122).
other: The word is of the same family as "submit", which this surah has built on: "When his Lord said to him, 'Submit,' he said, 'I have submitted to the Lord of the worlds'" (2:131; so also 2:112, 128, 133). A noun of the same Arabic letters is what people offer to God on the last day, their submission (16:28, 87). Believers are told the same thing elsewhere: "do not die except as people who submit" (3:102); "turn to your Lord and submit to him" (39:54). The verses just before set a man who will not give way when he is told "Be mindful of God" (2:206) beside one who "sells himself, seeking to please God" (2:207). And where the Quran says, "The religion with God is submission", it goes straight on to those who differed "out of resentment among themselves" (3:19), the words of 2:213.
review: open

## 2:219 great sin | much sin
kind: readings differ
call: clear
why: The main text always follows the Hafs reading, and it has "great". The sentence goes on with the same word: "their sin is greater than their benefit". It is the Quran's word for the weight of a wrong. Fighting in the sacred month is called by this same word two verses earlier, where the translation has "grave" (2:217); eating up the wealth of orphans "is a great sin" (4:2); killing children for fear of poverty "is a great wrong" (17:31); and the Quran speaks of "the great sins" (42:37; 53:32; so also 4:31).
other: Another established reading of the same letters has "much". That sets it beside "benefits", which is plural: much sin, and benefits. And the Quran counts more than one evil in these two things: they are "filth from Satan's work", through them Satan casts "enmity and hatred among you", and he bars people "from the remembrance of God and from the prayer" (5:90-91).
review: open
