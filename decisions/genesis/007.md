## 7:2 seven of each | seven pairs
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew says the number twice, "seven, seven", which is its way of saying "seven each". The numbers in this passage count animals, not couples. The same sentence goes on, "and of the beasts that are not clean, two, a male and its mate": the two are the male and the mate. The doubled number comes back as "two, two" ("two by two they came to Noah into the ark, male and female", 7:9; also 7:15), and that is two animals of each, as God had said: "you are to bring two of each into the ark ... They are to be male and female" (6:19). Elsewhere too a number said twice counts the things themselves: "the fingers of his hands and the toes of his feet were six and six, twenty-four in number" (2 Samuel 21:20); "you are to take five shekels each, by the head" (Numbers 3:47, where the Hebrew is "five, five shekels"). Read the same way, "seven, seven" is seven beasts of each, and "a male and its mate" says that both sexes are to be among them, as "male and female" says of the birds in the next verse (7:3).
other: In Hebrew "a male and its mate" is "a man and his wife", which names a couple, and seven animals cannot be made up of couples. So the thing counted seven times may be the couple: seven pairs, fourteen animals of each. In the second half of the sentence the same words stand beside "two", which is exactly one couple. The clean beasts too are then said to come "two by two ... male and female" (7:8-9), which fits seven couples better than seven animals, and the purpose is breeding: "to keep offspring alive on the face of the whole earth" (7:3). It is marked "ask" because the two readings give different numbers of animals, and the way Hebrew counts and the words "a male and its mate" pull in different directions. The same words stand in 7:3, which follows this call and has no bracket of its own.
