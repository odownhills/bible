## 48:12 he bowed down with his face to the ground | they bowed down to him with their faces to the ground
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text has "he bowed down" and "his face", and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. Joseph is the one the sentence is about ("Joseph brought them out from beside his knees"), and the man who holds power over Egypt bows to his father as Moses did to his father-in-law, "Moses went out to meet his father-in-law and bowed down and kissed him" (Exodus 18:7), and as King Solomon did to his mother, "the king rose to meet her and bowed down to her" (1 Kings 2:19).
other: The Samaritan copy and the old Greek have "they bowed down to him"; in Hebrew the two readings differ by one letter. Then it is the two boys, just brought out from their grandfather's knees, who bow to him, as Joseph's brothers "bowed down to him with their faces to the ground" (42:6).
review: open

## 48:14 he guided his hands with insight | he crossed his hands
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The verb is from the word that is about insight everywhere else in Scripture: the tree was "desirable for gaining insight" (3:6), "I will give you insight and teach you the way you should go" (Psalm 32:8), and with a part of the body, as here, "the heart of a wise man gives insight to his mouth" (Proverbs 16:23). The story says the same of what Israel did: Joseph takes it for a mistake, and his father answers, "I know, my son, I know" (48:19). The reason that follows fits it too: he had to guide his hands on purpose, "for Manasseh was the firstborn" and stood at his right hand (48:13).
other: The verse has just shown the hands crossing: Ephraim stood on Israel's left and Manasseh on his right (48:13), and the right hand went to Ephraim and the left to Manasseh. The verb in this form, said of hands, is found nowhere else, so no other verse settles it, and the old Greek and Latin versions put it as crossing or changing the hands. Read that way the last words are "though Manasseh was the firstborn". The hands are crossed on either reading; the question is only whether these words say so again or say that he did it knowingly.
review: open

## 48:22 one shoulder of land | Shechem, one portion
kind: both meant
call: clear
why: The Hebrew sets "one" beside the word, which makes it a thing that is counted and not a name, and as a common word it is "shoulder" everywhere else: the cloak "on the shoulders of the two of them" (9:23), Rebekah's jar "on her shoulder" (24:15). Jacob goes on to speak of it as ground won in war, "which I took from the hand of the Amorites with my sword and with my bow", and Scripture speaks of a slope of land as a shoulder, with its other word for one: the border "went up to the shoulder of the Jebusites on the south" (Joshua 15:8).
other: The same word is the name Shechem, the city where Jacob bought "the plot of the field where he had pitched his tent" (33:19) and where his sons came with the sword (34:25-29). Joseph's bones were buried "at Shechem, in the plot of the field that Jacob had bought", and it "became an inheritance of the sons of Joseph" (Joshua 24:32), and John speaks of "the plot of land that Jacob gave to Joseph his son" (John 4:5). So the name is heard in the word, and the bracketed note says that the one word carries both.
review: open
