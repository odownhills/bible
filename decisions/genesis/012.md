## 12:1 your relatives | your birthplace
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Hebrew word stands here by itself, between "your land" and "your father's house", and where this writer uses it by itself it means a person's kin. Abraham sends his servant for a wife for Isaac with the words "you are to go to my land and to my relatives" (24:4); the servant goes "to the city of Nahor" (24:10), not to Ur, and when he repeats the order he says "you are to go to my father's house and to my family" (24:38). Joseph's brothers say, "The man asked closely about us and about our relatives, saying, 'Is your father still alive? Do you have a brother?'" (43:7). Esther "had not told her people or her relatives" (Esther 2:10). And Stephen quotes this verse with a word that can only mean kin: "Go out from your land and from your relatives" (Acts 7:3).
other: With "the land of" in front of it the same word names the place a person comes from: Haran died "in the land of his birth, in Ur of the Chaldeans" (11:28), which is five verses before this one, and Jacob is told, "return to the land of your birth" (31:13). Abraham himself later looks back on this command in those words: Yahweh "took me from my father's house and from the land of my birth" (24:7). Read this way, the three things Abram is to leave are his land, the place where he was born, and his father's house.
review: open

## 12:2 and be a blessing | so that you will be a blessing
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb is a command, the same form as "Go" in 12:1, and the main text keeps it as one. This writer sets a command beside promises in the same way when Yahweh speaks to Abram about the covenant: "Walk before me and be blameless, and I will set my covenant between me and you" (17:1-2). So the speech has two commands, "Go" and "be a blessing", and each is followed by what Yahweh will do.
other: In Hebrew a command joined by "and" to what comes before it can state what is sure to follow: "he will pray for you, and live" (20:7), where the sense is "and you will live"; "Do this and live" (42:18). Here the command comes after three things that Yahweh says he will do, and the next verse explains the blessing as Yahweh's work, not Abram's: "I will bless those who bless you", "all the families of the ground will be blessed in you" (12:3). Elsewhere being a blessing is what God makes of a people: "so I will save you, and you will be a blessing" (Zechariah 8:13). Read this way, the words are a fourth promise and not a charge laid on Abram.
review: open

## 12:3 will be blessed in you | will bless themselves by you
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: All through the speech it is Yahweh who blesses ("I will bless you", "I will bless those who bless you"), and Abram is the one through whom it comes ("be a blessing", 12:2), so the families are the ones who receive. The rest of Scripture reads the words that way. Paul quotes them and builds on them: "'All the nations will be blessed in you.' So those who are of faith are blessed together with Abraham, who believed" (Galatians 3:8-9). Peter quotes the promise in the same sense: "And in your offspring all the families of the earth will be blessed" (Acts 3:25).
other: The Hebrew form of the verb here (also at 18:18 and 28:14) can mean that a thing is done to someone or that someone does it to himself. When the same promise is repeated at 22:18 and 26:4, the writer uses another form, which ordinarily means doing a thing to oneself: a man "blesses himself in his heart" (Deuteronomy 29:19), and "whoever blesses himself in the land will bless himself by the God of truth" (Isaiah 65:16). To bless "by" a person is to name him in the blessing: "By you Israel will bless, saying, 'May God make you like Ephraim and like Manasseh'" (48:20). Read this way, the families of the ground will wish for themselves what Abram has.
review: open
