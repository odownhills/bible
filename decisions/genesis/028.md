## 28:12 a stairway | a ladder
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The word stands only here in Scripture, so the only guide is the verb it is made from, which is the verb for building up a raised road: "they build up their way against me" (Job 19:12); "Build up, build up the highway" (Isaiah 62:10). The other nouns made from it are the highway one goes up by (Numbers 20:19; Isaiah 40:3) and the ramp heaped up against a city wall (2 Samuel 20:15). That points to a way built up from the ground, with steps rather than rungs. Many angels are going up and coming down on it at one time, and Jacob calls what he saw "the gate of the heavens" (28:17); the gates that Ezekiel sees are reached by steps (Ezekiel 40:6, 22).
other: It is "set up on the earth" and has a "top", as a thing that is stood on its foot, which is how a ladder is placed, and nothing in the verse speaks of steps. With no second place where the word is used, the root cannot settle it. Either one is a way up from the earth to the heavens.
review: open

## 28:13 beside him | above it
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew is one small word that can be "by him", "over him", "on it" or "above it". The sentences around it pull both ways (see the other line), so the writer's own use of the phrase decides: in Genesis "standing" with this small word is always standing by someone or something, never on top of it: "three men were standing near him" (18:2); "I am standing by the spring of water" (24:13, 43); "all who were standing by him" (45:1). Jacob is lying down, so one who stands by him stands over him. What he says on waking fits: "Surely Yahweh is in this place" (28:16), and he calls the place "the house of God" (28:17). Yahweh says, "I am with you" (28:15). Of a later night at this same place it is said, "God went up from him at the place where he had spoken with him" (35:13), and Yahweh "came and stood" by Samuel in the night (1 Samuel 3:10). If the top of the stairway were meant, "its top" has just been named (28:12) and could have been named again.
other: The stairway is the thing last spoken of, and the words just before say "its top" and "on it"; a hearer most easily takes the next small word of the stairway too. Then Yahweh stands above it in the heavens, where the angels go up to, and Jacob at its foot is at "the gate of the heavens" (28:17). The same two words are "on" a thing elsewhere: "you will stand on the rock" (Exodus 33:21). The old Greek has "on it". It is marked "ask" because the two readings give two pictures: Yahweh on the earth at Jacob's side, or Yahweh above the stairway.
review: open

## 28:22 then Yahweh will be God to me, and this stone that I have set up as a pillar will be the house of God | and Yahweh will be God to me, then this stone that I have set up as a pillar will be the house of God
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: Hebrew has no word for "then" here: the vow is one chain of "and"s after "If", and the hearer has to judge where the "if" ends (28:21 or 28:22). What Jacob asks for in 28:20-21 is what Yahweh has just promised him: "I am with you, and I will watch over you wherever you go, and I will bring you back to this ground" (28:15). Yahweh did not say there that he would be Jacob's God; he named himself "the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac" (28:13). So the first thing Jacob vows is that the God of his fathers will be his own God. The story goes on that way: while he is away he says "the God of my father" (31:5, 42; 32:9), and when he has come back safely he builds an altar and names it "God, the God of Israel" (33:18-20), and another at Bethel "to the God who answered me in the day of my distress and was with me on the way that I went" (35:3). Absalom's vow has this shape: "If Yahweh will indeed bring me back to Jerusalem, then I will serve Yahweh" (2 Samuel 15:8).
other: To "be God to" someone is elsewhere what God himself promises to do: "to be God to you and to your offspring after you ... and I will be God to them" (17:7-8), and that belongs to "the blessing of Abraham" that Isaac has just asked for Jacob (28:4). Read so, it is the last of the things Jacob hopes God will do for him, and what he vows is the stone and the tenth. The Hebrew also changes its shape at "and this stone": up to there every clause begins with its verb, and here the thing vowed is set first. It is marked "ask" because it decides whether Jacob made having Yahweh as his God a part of what he vowed, or one of the things he asked for.
review: open
