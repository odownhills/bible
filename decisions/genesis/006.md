## 6:3 My spirit will not remain in humankind forever | My spirit will not contend with humankind forever
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: This form of the Hebrew verb stands only here in Scripture, so the words around it have to decide, and they are about how long a human being lives: "because they too are flesh, and their days will be a hundred and twenty years". "The days of" someone "were" so many years is how chapter 5 gives the length of each life (5:4, 5, 8), and a hundred and twenty years is the age at which Moses dies (Deuteronomy 34:7). The spirit of God in a creature is what keeps it alive. This story says so: all flesh "in which there is the spirit of life" (6:17). Job says so with the words of this chapter: "if he gathered to himself his spirit and his breath, all flesh would breathe its last together, and a human being would return to dust" (Job 34:14-15); so also "the spirit of God is in my nostrils" (Job 27:3) and "you take away their spirit, they breathe their last" (Psalm 104:29). And Yahweh has spoken like this once before. The woman "saw" that the tree was "good" and "took" (3:6), as the sons of God do here (6:2), and Yahweh sent the human out "so that he does not ... live forever" (3:22), with the same word "forever". Where this writer uses the verb for judging, it is written with a different letter (49:16), which counts against taking this as that verb. The old Greek and Latin versions have "remain".
other: The verb looks like a form of the Hebrew verb for judging or arguing a case: "Dan will judge his people" (Genesis 49:16); "he cannot contend with one who is stronger than he" (Ecclesiastes 6:10). Read that way, Yahweh says that he will not go on contending with humankind, and the hundred and twenty years are the time left before the flood. Scripture speaks of God in this way: "For I will not contend forever, and I will not always be angry; for the spirit would grow faint before me" (Isaiah 57:16, with another verb for "contend"); "you bore with them many years and warned them by your spirit through your prophets" (Nehemiah 9:30); "the patience of God waited in the days of Noah" (1 Peter 3:20). The next lines are about the evil of humankind (6:5), and after the flood people still live much longer than a hundred and twenty years (11:10-26), which fits a time of waiting better than a length of life. It is marked "ask" because the verb stands nowhere else in this form, the two readings say different things about what God is doing, and both have Scripture on their side.
review: open

## 6:3 because they too are flesh | in their going astray they are flesh
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: With the vowels of the Hebrew text followed here, this is three small words written as one, "in that also". "Too" sets humankind beside the other creatures: in this story "all flesh" is every creature "in which there is the spirit of life" (6:17), and all of it breathes its last together (7:21-22). Flesh is Scripture's word for what does not last: "he remembered that they were flesh, a wind that goes and does not return" (Psalm 78:39); "the Egyptians are human and not God, and their horses are flesh and not spirit" (Isaiah 31:3). So the words give the reason for the limit on their days. The short word for "that" which this reading needs is rare, but it stands in old Hebrew such as the song of Deborah: "until I arose, Deborah" (Judges 5:7). The old Greek and Latin versions read the word this way ("because they are flesh", "because he is flesh").
other: Other Hebrew copies write one vowel of the word differently, and then it is "in their going astray", from the verb the Law uses for a sin done in error: "his error in which he went astray" (Leviticus 5:18). The short word for "that" stands nowhere else in Genesis, where "because" is said in another way (39:9, 23). The Hebrew has "he" for humankind in the rest of this verse, and "their" beside it is like verse 1, where humankind is "them". Read this way, the verse gives their wrongdoing as the reason, which is what the next lines go on to say (6:5).
review: open

## 6:4 The Nephilim | The giants, or the fallen ones
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: Scripture uses this word as the name of a people and does not say what it means. The only other place where it stands is the report of the spies, among the names of peoples: "And there we saw the Nephilim, the sons of Anak, who are from the Nephilim" (Numbers 13:33). The Law leaves the names of other peoples of great size as names in the same way: "The Emim lived there before, a people great and many and tall, like the Anakim" (Deuteronomy 2:10). So the name is written as it stands, as with the cherubim (3:24).
other: The old Greek version translates the word as "the giants", and that is what the spies say of them: "all the people that we saw in it are men of great size ... and we were like grasshoppers in our own eyes" (Numbers 13:32-33). The word also looks as if it is made from the Hebrew verb "fall", which would make it "the fallen ones". Ezekiel may be playing on that, with the other word of this verse beside it: "They do not lie with the mighty men, the fallen of the uncircumcised, who went down to Sheol with their weapons of war" (Ezekiel 32:27).
review: open

## 6:16 an opening for light for the ark | a roof for the ark
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew word stands only here. It is written with the letters of the word for "noon", the time of full light, which this writer uses ("for the men will eat with me at noon", 43:16), so it is read as something that lets light in. The story goes on to tell of such an opening: "Noah opened the window of the ark that he had made" (8:6), and nothing else in God's instructions could be that window. The Latin version has "a window" here.
other: "Window" in 8:6 is a different Hebrew word, so this word may be something else. The story also knows of a cover over the ark, "Noah removed the covering of the ark" (8:13), and the instructions name no roof unless this is it; a vessel that has to keep out forty days of rain (7:12) needs one. "Finish it to a cubit at the top" can be said of a roof as well as of an opening, and the old Greek version has no window here: it speaks of the ark being drawn together as it is finished at the top.
review: open
