## 14:1 king of Goiim | king of nations
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The other three kings in the sentence are each the king of a named land: Shinar (10:10; 11:2), Ellasar, and Elam (10:22). The fourth is written in the same way, with no "the" before the word, as a name is. Where Scripture speaks of a king over the nations as such, it says "the nations": "Who would not fear you, King of the nations?" (Jeremiah 10:7). And the same two Hebrew words are a name in the list of the kings whom Joshua struck down, where every other entry is the king of one town: "the king of Goiim in Gilgal, one" (Joshua 12:23).
other: The word is the ordinary Hebrew word for "nations", the one in "in their nations" (10:20) and in "from these the nations separated on the earth after the flood" (10:32), and apart from this chapter and Joshua 12:23 Scripture knows no land or people called Goiim. The word keeps its plain meaning inside the name of a region in "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1). Read this way, Tidal is a king over several peoples, and the verse does not say which.
review: open

## 14:4 and in the thirteenth year they rebelled | and for thirteen years they had rebelled
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The next verse counts on from this one: "And in the fourteenth year Chedorlaomer came" (14:5). Twelve, thirteen, fourteen is one count from one starting point: twelve years of service, the rebellion in the year after, and the king's answer in the year after that. If the rebellion had lasted thirteen years, "the fourteenth year" would have to be counted from a second starting point that the text never names. This is also how Scripture tells of a rebellion elsewhere: a number of years of service, and then the turn: "Jehoiakim became his servant for three years, and then he turned and rebelled against him" (2 Kings 24:1). The Samaritan copy of Genesis writes "and in the thirteenth year" in full here.
other: The Hebrew text has no "in" before "thirteen". Its words are "and thirteen years they rebelled", built exactly like the first half of the verse, "twelve years they served", where the number is a length of time. And when the writer means "in" a year he writes it, in the very next words: "And in the fourteenth year" (14:5). Read this way, the five kings served for twelve years and then stayed in rebellion for thirteen, and Chedorlaomer came in the fourteenth year of the rebellion.
review: open

## 14:19 owner of heaven and earth | who brought heaven and earth into being
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The chapter is about who owns what. The kings "took all the possessions of Sodom and Gomorrah" (14:11); Abram "brought back all the possessions" (14:16); "he gave him a tenth of everything" (14:20); the king of Sodom says, "the possessions take for yourself" (14:21). Abram answers by naming God in these same words and swearing that he will take nothing, "so that you will not say, 'I am the one who made Abram rich'" (14:22-23): he looks to the one who owns everything. Everywhere else in Genesis the verb means to get or to buy: "the field that Abraham bought from the sons of Heth" (25:10; also 33:19; 39:1; 47:19-23; 49:30; 50:13). And the form of the word used here, which names the one who does it, is elsewhere an owner or a buyer: "The ox knows its owner" (Isaiah 1:3); "the one who bought it" (Leviticus 25:28).
other: Where God is the one who does this verb, it can mean bringing into being: "Is he not your father, who brought you into being? He made you and set you firm" (Deuteronomy 32:6); "you brought my inmost parts into being; you wove me in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13); "Yahweh brought me into being at the beginning of his way" (Proverbs 8:22). Eve's words have the same two senses: "I have acquired a man with Yahweh" [alt: I have brought a man into being with Yahweh] (4:1). And a blessing built just like this one names God as maker: "Blessed are you by Yahweh, maker of heaven and earth" (Psalm 115:15; also 121:2; 124:8; 134:3). The bracket stands once; 14:22 has the same words and follows the same call.
review: open
