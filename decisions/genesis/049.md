## 49:5 their swords | their dealings, or their kinship
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew word stands only here in Scripture, so no other verse fixes its meaning. The sentence itself calls it "weapons of violence", and the next verse says what Jacob has in mind: "in their anger they killed a man". The story he looks back to is the one where "two of the sons of Jacob, Simeon and Levi, the brothers of Dinah, each took his sword" and "they killed every male", and Hamor and Shechem "they killed with the edge of the sword" (34:25-26). That the word means a sword rests on this sentence and on that story, not on another verse.
other: The word looks as if it comes from the verb "sell" (25:31; 37:28), whose noun is "wares" (Nehemiah 13:16). Then it is their dealings, and the word for "weapons" is also the word for tools: before the killing the two "answered Shechem and Hamor his father with deceit" (34:13) and set terms for marrying and trading (34:15-16, 21). It is also written almost like the word for the stock a people comes from: "Your origin and your birth are from the land of the Canaanites" (Ezekiel 16:3). Then the line answers "Simeon and Levi are brothers": what makes them kin is tools of violence.
review: open

## 49:6 they hamstrung an ox | they tore down a wall
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text as it is read has "ox", the old Greek has it too, and in the Old Testament the main text is always the Hebrew text. With these vowels the verb is what was done to animals taken in war: Joshua "hamstrung their horses" (Joshua 11:9), and David "hamstrung all the chariot horses" (2 Samuel 8:4).
other: With other vowels the same letters are "wall", as in "by my God I leap over a wall" (Psalm 18:29), and the verb is "uproot", as in "a time to plant and a time to uproot what is planted" (Ecclesiastes 3:2) and "Ekron will be uprooted" (Zephaniah 2:4). Old Aramaic and Latin versions read it so, of the city the two came against (34:25). The story does not tell of oxen being hamstrung: "Their flocks and their cattle and their donkeys, and what was in the city and what was in the field, they took" (34:28).
review: open

## 49:10 until Shiloh comes | until he comes to Shiloh, or until he comes to whom it belongs
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew text has the name "Shiloh" (its written and its read form differ only in spelling), and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. Taken as the one who comes, the name gives the next words someone to point to: "and the obedience of peoples will be his". Scripture itself seems to read the verse as a person coming to receive the rule. To the prince of Israel in his own day Ezekiel says, "Remove the turban and take off the crown ... A ruin, a ruin, a ruin I will make it. This too will not be, until he comes to whom the right belongs, and I will give it to him" (Ezekiel 21:26-27). And Judah's scepter was the house of David (2 Samuel 7:16; Psalm 78:68-70), which came after God "abandoned the dwelling place at Shiloh" (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14), so a scepter that lasts "until he comes to Shiloh" does not fit the story as Scripture tells it.
other: Everywhere else in Scripture Shiloh is a town and never a person: "the whole congregation of the sons of Israel assembled at Shiloh and set up the tent of meeting there, and the land was subdued before them" (Joshua 18:1). The same two Hebrew words mean "came to Shiloh" at 1 Samuel 4:12. Read so, Judah goes first ("Judah will go up", Judges 1:2) until Israel comes to Shiloh. The second alternate is a matter of old copies: the Samaritan copy writes the word with one letter fewer, and the old Greek, Syriac and Aramaic versions took it as "what is his" or "he whose it is". That is how Ezekiel 21:27 words it, with no name.
review: open

## 49:12 dark from wine, white from milk | darker than wine, whiter than milk
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The verse before is about wine in plenty: he ties his colt to the vine and "washes his clothing in wine". The only other place where Scripture has this word for the eyes says that wine is what causes it: "Who has darkness of eyes? Those who linger over wine" (Proverbs 23:29-30). So the eyes are dark from wine and the teeth white from milk: the plenty of "a land flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8).
other: The same small Hebrew word is the ordinary way to say "more than": "his younger brother will be greater than he" (48:19). And Scripture praises looks in just these words: "Her consecrated ones were purer than snow, whiter than milk" (Lamentations 4:7). Read so, the verse says how fine he looks, not how much he has.
review: open

## 49:21 he gives beautiful words | a doe that bears beautiful fawns, or a spreading tree that puts out beautiful branches
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew text as it is read has "doe" and "words", and the word for "words" is the ordinary one: "upright words" (Job 6:25), "the words of my mouth" (Psalm 19:14). The one who "gives" is in the masculine, so it is Naphtali who gives, not the doe, and Scripture speaks of giving words: "The Lord gives the word" (Psalm 68:11).
other: A doe is an odd picture for one who speaks. The word for "words" is written like an Aramaic word for lambs (Ezra 6:9, 17), which would be her young. With other vowels "doe" is the word for a large tree, the terebinth (35:4); "let loose" is the verb used of the vine that "sent out its branches to the sea" (Psalm 80:11); the word for "words" is written like the word for a top branch (Isaiah 17:6); and a tree "gives its fruit" (Psalm 1:3). The old Greek took the saying as a picture of a tree.
review: open

## 49:22 a fruitful bough, its branches climb over a wall | a wild donkey's colt, its young stride along by Shur
kind: two senses
call: close
why: "Fruitful" is the word Genesis has already tied to Joseph: he named his son Ephraim "because God has made me fruitful in the land of my affliction" (41:52), and Jacob has just said of Joseph's sons, "may they teem into a multitude in the midst of the land" (48:16). Scripture uses this word of a vine: "Your wife will be like a fruitful vine" (Psalm 128:3); "Your mother was like a vine ... fruitful and full of branches because of many waters" (Ezekiel 19:10). It calls a vine's shoot a "son" in a psalm that names Joseph, Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh (Psalm 80:1-2, 15), and it has "by my God I leap over a wall" (Psalm 18:29). Moses' blessing of Joseph is about the fruit of the land as well (Deuteronomy 33:13-16).
other: The first word is written much like the word for a wild donkey, which Scripture uses of a man, "a wild donkey of a man" (16:12), and of Ephraim, "a wild donkey alone by itself" (Hosea 8:9). The sayings on either side are pictures of animals (a doe, a wolf), archers come next (49:23), and "by a spring" and "by Shur" (the word for "wall" is also that place name) are the words of 16:7, "by the spring on the way to Shur". But Scripture nowhere has a feminine form of "wild donkey", and the last line is hard on either reading: in the Hebrew it is "daughters stepped over a wall".
review: open

## 49:26 over the blessings of my parents, as far as the delight of the everlasting hills | over the blessings of the enduring mountains, the delight of the everlasting hills
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text has "my parents, as far as", and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. Jacob sets the blessings he gives beside those he was given, the blessing of Isaac his father (27:27-29; 28:3-4). "Delight" is the word of 3:6, "a delight to the eyes", and it means what one desires everywhere else in Scripture.
other: With one letter changed the words are "the enduring mountains", and the old Greek has "mountains". Scripture has that pair elsewhere: "the enduring mountains were shattered, the everlasting hills sank low" (Habakkuk 3:6), and Moses' blessing of Joseph, which follows this one closely, has "the best of the ancient mountains" beside "the choice things of the everlasting hills" (Deuteronomy 33:15). On either reading the word for "delight" could instead be "boundary", from the verb "mark out" (Numbers 34:10): "as far as the bounds of the everlasting hills".
review: open
