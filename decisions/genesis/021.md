## 21:6 will laugh with me | will laugh at me
kind: two senses
call: close
why: Sarah names the laughter as something God has given her, "Laughter God has made for me", just after "Yahweh did for Sarah as he had spoken" (21:1), and she goes on in wonder, not in shame: "Who would have said to Abraham, 'Sarah has nursed children'?" (21:7). The name itself was God's choice (17:19). Where Yahweh gives back what was lost, laughter and the talk of those who hear of it go together: "Then our mouth was filled with laughter, and our tongue with a cry of joy; then they said among the nations, 'Yahweh has done great things for them'" (Psalm 126:2); "until he fills your mouth with laughter" (Job 8:21). When Elizabeth bore a son in her old age, "her neighbors and her relatives heard that the Lord had made his mercy to her great, and they rejoiced with her" (Luke 1:58).
other: The Hebrew is "will laugh for me", and with this verb, in its other spelling, those words are elsewhere laughing at something: "At destruction and starvation you will laugh" (Job 5:22); "she laughs at the horse and its rider" (Job 39:18); "But you, Yahweh, laugh at them" (Psalm 59:8). The only other place that has this word for "laughter" is scorn: "for laughter and for mockery" (Ezekiel 23:32). Abraham laughed (17:17) and Sarah laughed (18:12) at the thought of a birth at their age, and Sarah may be saying that everyone who hears of an old woman nursing will do the same at her.
review: open

## 21:9 laughing | mocking
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The word stands alone, with no "at" after it and no one named, and standing alone it is play or jest: "he was like one who was joking" (19:14); "the people sat down to eat and to drink, and they rose to play" (Exodus 32:6). It is the verb of Isaac's own name, at Isaac's feast (21:8), three verses after "everyone who hears will laugh" (21:6). What Sarah gives as her reason is not anything the boy did but who he is: "the son of this female servant will not be heir with my son, with Isaac" (21:10); and the writer has just called him "the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham". Nothing in the chapter blames him: the thing is "very bad" in Abraham's eyes (21:11), God calls him "your offspring" (21:13), hears his voice (21:17) and is with him (21:20). The old Greek adds "with Isaac her son" and takes the word as play.
other: With "at" this same verb is mocking: "he has brought us a Hebrew man to mock us" (39:14, and 39:17). Paul speaks of this day as an attack: "just as then the one born according to the flesh persecuted the one born according to the spirit, so it is now too" (Galatians 4:29). Sarah's demand, "Drive out this female servant and her son", follows at once, as if she had seen a wrong done; and Hagar had once looked down on her: "her mistress counted for little in her eyes" (16:4).
review: open

## 21:14 putting them on her shoulder, and he gave her the child | putting them on her shoulder, and the child too
kind: grammar
call: clear
why: Ishmael is not an infant. Abram was eighty-six when he was born (16:16) and a hundred when Isaac was born (21:5); "Ishmael his son was thirteen years old when he was circumcised" (17:25), a year before Isaac's birth (17:21); and Isaac has now been weaned (21:8). The angel tells Hagar, "lift up the boy and hold him fast with your hand" (21:18), and the same chapter calls him "the boy" six times (21:12, 17-20). In the Hebrew "the child" carries the mark of a direct object, as one more thing that Abraham "took" and "gave", and "putting them on her shoulder" stands between as an aside about the bread and the water.
other: "And the child" comes right after "on her shoulder", so the words can be put together as one act: he put the bread, the skin and the child on her shoulder. The old Greek has it so: "and he put the child on her shoulder". The next verses can be heard as speaking of a small child: she "threw the child under one of the shrubs" (21:15) and sat far off so as not to look on his death (21:16).
review: open

## 21:16 and lifted her voice and wept | and the child cried out and wept, as the old Greek has it
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text has "she" in both verbs, and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. Hagar is the one who acts all through the verse: she went, she sat down, she said, she sat. The same words tell of a person's own weeping: "they lifted their voice and wept" (Job 2:12); "And Esau lifted his voice and wept" (27:38). And God hears what the story has not put into words: "Yahweh has heard your affliction" (16:11), where no cry of Hagar's had been told; "The voice of your brother's blood is crying out to me from the ground" (4:10).
other: The next words are "And God heard the voice of the boy", and the angel says it again, "God has heard the voice of the boy where he is" (21:17); in the Hebrew text nothing has said that the boy made a sound. In the old Greek it is the child who cries out and weeps here, so that what God hears has just been told, and Hagar only sits.
review: open
