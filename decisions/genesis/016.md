## 16:9 and let yourself be afflicted under her hands | and humble yourself under her hands
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The same Hebrew verb has just been used of what Sarai did: "Sarai afflicted her, and she fled from her" (16:6), and the angel goes on to name what Yahweh has heard with the noun from the same root, "your affliction" (16:11); so the command sends Hagar back under the very thing she fled. It is also the verb of 15:13, "they will afflict them, four hundred years". This form of the verb is what a person undergoes at 1 Kings 2:26 ("you were afflicted in all that my father was afflicted in") and Psalm 107:17 ("because of their guilt they were afflicted").
other: This form of the verb is the one for what a person does to himself, and twice it is said of people lowering themselves before God, with fasting: "to humble ourselves before our God" (Ezra 8:21); "you set your heart to understand and to humble yourself before your God" (Daniel 10:12). "Under her hands" can be heard as under her authority, as Abram has just said, "your female slave is in your hand" (16:6). Read this way the angel tells Hagar to put herself under her mistress again and says nothing of how she will be treated.
review: open

## 16:12 in the presence of all his brothers | in defiance of all his brothers
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The verb is "live", a word for where a person has his home, and the Hebrew phrase, "at the face of", is said of a person in whose sight something is: "Haran died in the presence of Terah his father" (11:28). When Genesis tells how this word came about, the phrase stands first for the lie of the land and then, in the same sentence, with "all his brothers" again: "they lived from Havilah as far as Shur, which is opposite Egypt as you go toward Asshur; he settled in the presence of all his brothers" (25:18). His brothers are the sons whom Abraham sent "eastward, to the land of the east" (25:6), and Isaac, with whom he buried their father (25:9).
other: The words just before are about fighting: "his hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him". The same phrase is also said of what is done to a person's face, against him: "he will certainly curse you to your face" (Job 1:11); "he may not treat the son of the loved one as the firstborn in the face of the son of the hated one, who is the firstborn" (Deuteronomy 21:16). And the verb of 25:18, "he settled", is the word "fell", which is said of armies lying in a valley before a battle (Judges 7:12) and of raiders: "Sheba fell on them" (Job 1:15).
review: open

## 16:13 Have I even here seen the back of the one who sees me? | Have I even here gone on seeing, after the one who sees me?
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: Hagar gives this as her reason for the name "a God of seeing", and the reason has both sides of it: she has seen, and he sees her. The Hebrew word between the two is "behind" or "after". It is the hind part of a thing at 2 Samuel 2:23 ("struck him in the belly with the back end of the spear"), and with a verb of looking it is where one looks when someone is going away: "they would gaze after Moses until he had gone into the tent" (Exodus 33:8). That is what Scripture says a person may see of Yahweh, in a word from the same root: "you will see my back, but my face will not be seen" (Exodus 33:23). The old Latin version has "the back of the one who sees me" here.
other: The same word is most often "after" in time: "after he fathered Seth" (5:4). Read so, Hagar marvels that she still has her sight, that is, still lives, after God has looked on her, for those who see God expect to die: "I have seen God face to face, and my life has been rescued" (32:30); "We will certainly die, for we have seen God" (Judges 13:22); "no human being can see me and live" (Exodus 33:20). The name given to the well sets "living" beside "seeing": "the well of the living one who sees me" (16:14).
review: open
