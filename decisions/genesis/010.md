## 10:4 the Dodanim | the Rodanim
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text of Genesis reads "Dodanim", and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. Scripture names this people nowhere else, apart from the list in Chronicles that repeats this one, so nothing else in Scripture can confirm the name or correct it. (The "Dedan" of 10:7 is another name, a grandson of Cush.)
other: Where this list is repeated, the Hebrew text itself reads "Rodanim": "And the sons of Javan were Elishah and Tarshish, the Kittim and the Rodanim" (1 Chronicles 1:7). The Samaritan copy of Genesis has the same name here, and the old Greek version has "Rhodians", the people of the island of Rhodes (Acts 21:1). An island people fits the next sentence, "From these the coastland nations separated" (10:5), and the names beside it: Scripture speaks of "the coastlands of Elishah" (Ezekiel 27:7) and "the coastlands of the Kittim" (Jeremiah 2:10). In Hebrew writing the letters for D and R look almost alike, so one of the two names came from the other in copying.
review: open

## 10:11 From that land he went out to Asshur | From that land Asshur went out
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew words are "from that land went out Asshur", and Asshur can be the place he went to or the one who went. The lines before are all about Nimrod: "He began to be a mighty man on the earth" (10:8); "He was a mighty hunter before Yahweh" (10:9); "the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon ... in the land of Shinar" (10:10). A "beginning" looks ahead to what came next, and with "he" this sentence gives it: from Shinar he went on to Asshur and built Nineveh. Micah sets the two names side by side for one country: "they will shepherd the land of Asshur with the sword, and the land of Nimrod at its entrances" (Micah 5:6). Hebrew can name the place a person goes to with no word for "to", and does so with this very name: "they call to Egypt, they go to Asshur" (Hosea 7:11); "for they have gone up to Asshur" (Hosea 8:9); and with this verb, "go out to the field" (Genesis 27:3). Asshur the son of Shem is not named until 10:22, and this part of the chapter is the line of Ham.
other: The plainest way to take the Hebrew words is with Asshur as the one who went out, and this chapter speaks of a people in just that way three verses later: "from whom the Philistines came out" (10:14). Where this writer means "toward" a place he usually marks it with an ending on the name, as he does three times in this chapter (10:19, 30) and with this name itself, "as you go toward Asshur" (25:18); here the name has no such ending. Asshur is a person in this chapter, a son of Shem (10:22), and Scripture speaks of the people by his name: "Asshur also has joined them" (Psalm 83:8). Read this way, the sentence says that Asshur left Nimrod's land and built Nineveh and the other cities himself. It is marked "ask" because the two readings name different builders of Nineveh, Nimrod of the line of Ham or Asshur of the line of Shem, and the Hebrew words allow both.
review: open

## 10:21 the brother of Japheth the eldest | the older brother of Japheth
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew words are "brother of Japheth the big one", and "the big one" can go with Japheth or with "brother". Scripture's own numbers show that Shem was not the firstborn. "Noah was five hundred years old, and Noah fathered Shem, Ham, and Japheth" (5:32), and "Noah was six hundred years old when the flood of water came on the earth" (7:6); so the first of his sons was a hundred years old in the year of the flood. But Shem was "a hundred years old" when he fathered Arpachshad "two years after the flood" (11:10), so Shem was born at least two years after the first. And Ham is called Noah's "youngest son" (9:24). That leaves Japheth as the eldest. The order "Shem, Ham, and Japheth" does not show their ages: Scripture often names the brother who matters most to the story first, as in "Moses and Aaron", though Aaron was three years older (Exodus 7:7), and this chapter itself takes Japheth first and Shem last. The accent marks of the Hebrew text also join "the big one" to "Japheth" and set "brother of" apart from the two.
other: When this word follows "son" or "brother" it most often describes that relative: "Esau his elder son" (27:1); "Eliab his eldest brother" (1 Samuel 17:28); and with a name standing between, as here, "Othniel the son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother" (Judges 1:13). Shem is named first of the three every time (5:32; 6:10; 7:13; 9:18; 10:1), and Noah's blessing names him before Japheth (9:26-27). Read this way, the words say that Shem, whose line is told last, is the older of the two, and the five hundred years of 5:32 have to be taken as a round number. It is marked "ask" because it decides which of Noah's sons this verse calls the eldest, and the published wording of 9:24, "his youngest son", rests on the same reckoning.
review: open

## 10:24 fathered Shelah | fathered Kenan, and Kenan fathered Shelah
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Hebrew text has no one between Arpachshad and Shelah, and in the Old Testament the main text is always that text. The Hebrew says the same wherever this line is told again: "And Arpachshad lived thirty-five years, and he fathered Shelah" (11:12); "And Arpachshad fathered Shelah, and Shelah fathered Eber" (1 Chronicles 1:18); "Shem, Arpachshad, Shelah" (1 Chronicles 1:24).
other: The old Greek version has one more generation here, and again at 11:12-13, where it gives this Kenan's years. Luke's list of the forefathers of Jesus has it too: "of Eber, of Shelah, of Kenan, of Arpachshad, of Shem" (Luke 3:35-36). The name is the one that stands earlier in the same line as the son of Enosh (Genesis 5:9; Luke 3:37); in English it is often written "Cainan".
review: open
