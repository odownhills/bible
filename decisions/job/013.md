## 13:15 I will not wait | I will wait for him
kind: copies differ
call: ask
why: The written Hebrew text has "not", and the rules follow the written text wherever it can be read; here it can. It is what Job says of waiting and hoping again and again: "What is my strength, that I should wait?" (6:11); "they come to an end without hope" (7:6); "Where then is my hope?" (17:15); "I waited for light, and deep darkness came" (30:26). He has just said that he is putting his life in his hand, come what may (13:13-14), and he expects to die: "I will now lie down in the dust" (7:21). The "but" that follows then says that he will argue his ways all the same.
other: The traditional way of reading the verse aloud has "for him" where the written text has "not"; the two words sound the same in Hebrew. "I will wait for him" is what is said of God elsewhere, with the same verb: "Yahweh is my portion, says my soul; that is why I will wait for him" (Lamentations 3:24; also Psalm 38:15; Micah 7:7). The next line speaks of salvation (13:16), and later in this same speech Job does speak of waiting: "All the days of my soldier's service I would wait, until my relief came" (14:14). The written text and the reading aloud differ over these same two words in other places (6:21; Psalm 100:3; Isaiah 63:9).
review: open

## 13:16 This too will be my salvation | He too will be my salvation
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew pronoun can be "this" or "he". The reason Job gives is about coming before God: "for a godless man does not come before him". So "this" is what he has just said he will do, argue his ways "to his face" (13:15); in Hebrew "before him" has the same word, "face", as "to his face". That he dares to come before God is itself what will save him, and he goes on: "I have set out my case; I know that I myself will be found righteous" (13:18). Job uses the same two words, "this too", of a thing and not of a person: "this too would be guilt calling for judgment" (31:28).
other: "He has become my salvation" is what Scripture says of God, with the same words for "be my salvation": "Yah is my strength and song, and he has become my salvation" (Exodus 15:2; also Psalm 118:14; Isaiah 12:2). "He" at the head of the line would then be the same person as "him" at its end, and the one who will kill Job (13:15) would also be the one who saves him, as Eliphaz said, "he wounds, and his hands heal" (5:18), and as Job says later: "Even now, look, my witness is in the heavens" (16:19).
review: open

## 13:27 you draw a line around the soles of my feet | you cut a mark into the soles of my feet
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb (in this form it stands only here) is from a word for cutting or inscribing, and so for marking out a limit. Job uses that word of the limits God sets: "you have set his limit, and he cannot pass it" (14:5); "He has drawn a circle on the face of the waters" (26:10; also Proverbs 8:27, 29). Yahweh says that he put "my limit" on the sea together with "a bar and doors" (38:10): a limit beside things that shut in, as here it stands beside the stocks. Job says the same of himself elsewhere: "He has walled up my path, and I cannot pass" (19:8).
other: The same word is used of cutting a figure into a surface, with the same "on" as here: "engraved on the wall all around" (Ezekiel 8:10); "I have engraved you on my palms" (Isaiah 49:16); and Job wishes his words were "inscribed in a book" (19:23). Read that way, God cuts his mark into the soles of Job's feet, as an owner's name is written on the hand (Isaiah 44:5), so that every step can be traced. That fits the words just before, "you keep watch on all my paths", and what Job says next of God: "you count my steps" (14:16).
review: open
