## 7:5 runs | is loathsome
kind: two senses
call: close
why: Two Hebrew verbs are spelled alike here. One means to run or flow away, and it stands in this same form in "let them run off like water that flows away" (Psalm 58:7). Job is describing what is happening to his body, one thing after another: his flesh is clothed with maggots and clods of dust, his skin breaks out, and then it runs. The verb before it is the one for the sea being stirred up "so that its waves roar" (Isaiah 51:15; Jeremiah 31:35; compare Job 26:12), so the skin erupts and then discharges.
other: The other verb is "reject", and it is the one found everywhere else in this book: "do not reject the discipline of the Almighty" (5:17), "I reject my life" (9:21), "even young children reject me" (19:18). In this same form it means to be rejected or despised: "a wife of one's youth when she is rejected" (Isaiah 54:6), "rejected silver" (Jeremiah 6:30). On this reading his skin breaks out and is loathsome, and the word comes back when he says, "I reject my life" (7:16).
review: open

## 7:6 without hope | for lack of thread
kind: both meant
call: clear
why: The Hebrew word is the usual one for hope, and hope is what Job goes on to speak of: "my eye will not see good again" (7:7). It is the noun of the verb he has just used of the hired worker who "hopes for his pay" (7:2), and it means hope all through the book: "your hope the blamelessness of your ways" (4:6), "what I hope for" (6:8), "there is hope for a tree" (14:7), "where then is my hope?" (17:15).
other: The same word is the "cord" of scarlet thread that Rahab tied in her window (Joshua 2:18, 21), and the first half of the line is about weaving: "swifter than a weaver's shuttle". So the days also run out the way weaving stops when the thread gives out, as "for lack of wood the fire goes out" (Proverbs 26:20, with the same small Hebrew word before "wood"). The bracketed note says that the one word carries both.
review: open

## 7:20 If I have sinned, what do I do to you | I have sinned; what am I to do for you
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew has only "I have sinned", with no word for "if", but Hebrew can set a supposition down bare: "You have found honey: eat only what you need" (Proverbs 25:16). Elihu takes up these very words with an "if": "If you have sinned, what do you do to him?" (35:6), and Job himself says, "If I sin, you watch me" (10:14). In this same speech Job has asked to be shown any fault, "make me understand how I have gone astray" (6:24), and he goes on saying that he is blameless (9:21) and that God knows he is not guilty (10:7). So he grants a sin for the sake of argument: even then, what harm does it do to God, and why not forgive it (7:21)?
other: Taken as it stands, it is a confession, and that is what this word is in a speaker's mouth elsewhere: Pharaoh (Exodus 9:27), Achan (Joshua 7:20), Saul (1 Samuel 15:24), David (2 Samuel 12:13), and the restored man in this book, "I sinned and twisted what was right" (33:27). The next verse speaks of "my rebellion" and "my guilt" as things that are there to be taken away, and Job owns to "the guilty deeds of my youth" (13:26). Then the question that follows asks what he could do to put it right, as in "With what shall I come before Yahweh?" (Micah 6:6).
review: open

## 7:20 a burden to myself | a burden to you
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Masoretic Text has "to myself", and in the Old Testament the main text is always the Masoretic Text. It fits what Job has just said: his soul chooses death rather than his bones, and he rejects his life (7:15-16); and he says it again, "My soul loathes my life" (10:1).
other: The old Greek version and a few Hebrew copies have "to you", and the Hebrew scribes themselves handed down a note that this is one of a small number of places where the wording was changed out of reverence for God. "To you" matches "what do I do to you" and "your target" in the same verse, and it leads into the next question, why God does not carry the load away (7:21). Elsewhere the phrase is used of being a burden to someone else: "you will be a burden to me" (2 Samuel 15:33; so also 19:35).
review: open
