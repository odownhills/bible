## 16:8 you have shriveled me up | you have seized me
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb stands only here and at 22:16. The rest of the line is about what can be seen on Job's body: "it has become a witness, and my leanness has risen against me". A shriveled body and leanness are one witness named twice, the way the two halves of a line in Job answer each other. Job points to his wasted body in the same way elsewhere: "My bone clings to my skin and to my flesh" (19:20); "all my limbs are like a shadow" (17:7). And he has already called what God does to him God's witnesses: "You renew your witnesses against me" (10:17).
other: At 22:16, the only other place where this verb stands, Eliphaz says it of the men of old whose foundation a river washed away: they "were seized before their time". That gives "you have seized me" here, and the old Greek version has it so ("you laid hold of me"). The lines around are a run of attacks on Job: "you have devastated all my company" (16:7); "His anger has torn me" (16:9); "he seized me by the back of the neck" (16:12, with another verb). Read that way, it is God's laying hold of him that has become the witness.
review: open

## 16:8 my leanness has risen against me | the lie told about me has risen against me
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew noun comes from a verb that is used of a body wasting away: "my flesh has grown lean" (Psalm 109:24). The line has just spoken of Job's body ("you have shriveled me up"), and his body is what everyone can see: "My bone clings to my skin and to my flesh" (19:20). So his leanness is the witness that "testifies to my face", like the witnesses that God renews against him (10:17).
other: Everywhere else in Scripture this noun means lying (Hosea 7:3; 10:13; 11:12; Nahum 3:1; Psalm 59:12), and the old Greek version has "my lie" here. "Rise against" and "testify" are the words Scripture uses of a false witness: "If a malicious witness rises against a man to testify against him" (Deuteronomy 19:16); "false witnesses have risen against me" (Psalm 27:12; also 35:11). The next lines turn to people who gape at Job and strike him (16:10), and he goes on to say that there is "no violence in my palms" (16:17), which is the word used of that witness ("a witness of violence") in Deuteronomy 19:16 and Psalm 35:11.
review: open

## 16:11 to the unjust | to a child
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The second half of the line says the same thing with "the wicked": "into the hands of the wicked he hurls me". Job sets these two side by side elsewhere, with a word from the same root as this one: "Let my enemy be like the wicked, and the one who rises against me like the unjust" (27:7; the same word for the unjust at 18:21; 29:17; 31:3). The root is that of "injustice" (6:29-30; 11:14; 15:16). Being handed over to enemies is what this verb is used for elsewhere: "you have not handed me over into the hand of the enemy" (Psalm 31:8). The old Greek version has "the unjust" here.
other: The word as it is written here stands in only two other places in Scripture, both in Job, and both times it means young children: "Even young children reject me" (19:18); "They send out their young children like a flock" (21:11). The word for "the unjust" at 27:7 is written differently. Job's complaint in these lines is that he is mocked and struck (16:10), and he says the same of the young elsewhere: "But now they laugh at me, those younger than I in days" (30:1). To be given over to children is a judgment in Isaiah 3:4: "I will make boys their princes".
review: open

## 16:20 My friends scoff at me | My spokesman is my friend
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: As the Masoretic Text points them, both words are plural: "my friends" and "those who scoff at me". In Job's mouth "my friends" are the three who have come to him: "Have pity on me, have pity on me, you my friends" (19:21); he is "a laughingstock to his friend" (12:4). The verb is "scoff" with the same object, "me", in Psalm 119:51: "The arrogant have scoffed at me greatly" (also Proverbs 3:34; 19:28). Job has just called the three "comforters who bring misery" (16:2), and he goes straight on with the same two things, those who mock him and his eye: "Surely mockers are with me, and my eye rests on their provocation" (17:2). The Hebrew sets "to God" first in the second half of the line: his friends scoff, so it is to God that he weeps.
other: In every other place in Scripture this form of the word is not a scoffer but one who speaks between two parties: the interpreter between Joseph and his brothers (Genesis 42:23), the envoys of the princes of Babylon (2 Chronicles 32:31), Israel's spokesmen (Isaiah 43:27), and in Job itself a heavenly one who speaks for a man: "an angel, a spokesman, one of a thousand" (33:23). With other vowels the same letters are singular: "My spokesman is my friend". The line stands between "my witness is in the heavens, and the one who testifies for me is on high" (16:19) and "that he would argue for a man with God, as a human being does for his friend" (16:21), where a friend is one who takes a man's side.
review: open

## 16:21 as a human being does for his friend | and decide between a human being and his friend
kind: grammar
call: close
why: As the Masoretic Text points it, the word is "son of": "a son of man", that is, a human being. That is the usual partner, in the second half of a line, of a word for a man in the first half (25:6; 35:8; Numbers 23:19; Psalm 8:4), and the old Greek version has "a son of man" here too. The same small word, "for", stands before "a man" and before "his friend", so that each is the one who is argued for; and an "and" that sets a picture beside a saying is "as" (5:7; 12:11). Job has just said what his own friends do (16:20), and he has said what a friend owes: "One who is losing heart is owed loyalty by his friend" (6:14).
other: With other vowels the same letters are "and between", and "between a man and his friend" is how Scripture speaks of settling a dispute: "I judge between a man and his friend" (Exodus 18:16; also Deuteronomy 1:16; Jeremiah 7:5; and Ezekiel 18:8, where "between" is followed by the same small word as here). Read that way Job asks for two rulings, one between himself and God and one between himself and his friends; and the verb is the one behind "arbiter": "There is no arbiter between us" (9:33); "let them decide between us two" (Genesis 31:37).
review: open
