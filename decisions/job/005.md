## 5:5 the snare pants for their wealth | the thirsty pant for their wealth
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Masoretic Text has a word for a snare, and the main text keeps to it. Bildad uses the same word in his list of the traps that catch the wicked man: "a snare holds him fast" (18:9). The verb here is singular, as "snare" needs and as it is with this word in 18:9, and it is the verb of eager craving, "as a slave pants for the shadow" (7:2); so the snare is pictured as greedy for the family's wealth, as Sheol "has opened its mouth beyond measure" (Isaiah 5:14).
other: With one silent letter left unwritten, the same letters spell "the thirsty", and several of the ancient versions read it that way. It matches the first line of the verse, "the hungry eat his harvest": the hungry and the thirsty stand side by side in Isaiah 29:8, Isaiah 32:6 and Psalm 107:5.
review: open

## 5:7 is born to misery | begets misery
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: With the vowels the Masoretic Text gives it, the verb is passive, "is born", the form in "to Seth also a son was born" (Genesis 4:26), and the main text keeps to the Masoretic vowels. Job says nearly the same: "A human being, born of a woman, is short of days and full of turmoil" (14:1), and Eliphaz asks later, "What is a mortal, that he should be pure, and one born of a woman, that he should be righteous?" (15:14). The comparison fits it: misery comes with being born human as surely as sparks go up.
other: With other vowels the same letters are "begets", the verb of "he fathered sons and daughters" (Genesis 5:4). That follows closely from verse 6: harm and misery do not grow out of the ground; people produce them. It is what Eliphaz has just said, "those who plow harm and sow misery harvest it" (4:8), and what he says later with the same two words: "They conceive misery and give birth to harm" (15:35; so also Psalm 7:14; Isaiah 59:4). On this reading suffering is traced to what people do, not to their being born.
review: open

## 5:17 the Almighty | Shaddai
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: This is the first of thirty-one places in Job where God is called by this Hebrew word, and it will be written the same way in all of them. It is set beside "God" as a title, as here, and Scripture ties it to overwhelming power: "like destruction from the Almighty it will come" (Isaiah 13:6; Joel 1:15), where the Hebrew word for "destruction" (the one in 5:21-22) sounds like it; "the Almighty, we cannot find him; he is great in power" (37:23). "The Almighty" is also a title of God in the New Testament (2 Corinthians 6:18; Revelation 1:8; 4:8). The rules put the divine names into English where English has a word ("God", "Lord") and keep only the name Yahweh.
other: The Hebrew word never takes "the", as a name does not, and God gives it as a name of his own: "I am God Shaddai" (Genesis 17:1; 35:11); "I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob as God Shaddai, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them" (Exodus 6:3). Naomi sets it beside the name Yahweh (Ruth 1:20-21). Scripture nowhere says what the word means, so "the Almighty" gives it a meaning the text itself does not state; "Shaddai" would treat it as "Yahweh" is treated.
review: open

## 5:24 find nothing missing | you will not sin
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The first sense of the Hebrew verb is to miss: men who could "sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judges 20:16), and "the one who misses me" set against "the one who finds me" (Proverbs 8:35-36). The line is about a man going over what he owns: the verb for "inspect" is the one for counting people and animals, and when a count comes up short they are "missing" by a form of that same verb (Numbers 31:49; 1 Samuel 25:7, 15, 21). The lines on either side are promises of safety and plenty: "your tent is at peace", "your offspring are many" (5:24-25).
other: Everywhere else in Job this verb is "sin": "Perhaps my sons have sinned" (1:5), "Job did not sin" (1:22; 2:10), "If I have sinned" (7:20; also 8:4; 10:14; 33:27; 35:6). On that reading Eliphaz promises that Job will look after his home and be kept from doing wrong.
review: open

## 5:26 in full vigor | in ripe old age
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew word stands in only one other place in Scripture, and there it is set beside bodily strength: "What use to me was the strength of their hands? Vigor had perished from them" (30:2). So the promise is that Job will reach the grave with his strength still in him, which Job himself counts a good death: "One dies in his full strength, wholly untroubled and at ease" (21:23). A stack of grain brought in at harvest is full and firm.
other: The comparison that follows is about time, "as a stack of grain goes up in its season", which points to a life that has reached its full length. That is the promise elsewhere: "you will be buried at a good old age" (Genesis 15:15); Abraham "died at a good old age, old and full" (Genesis 25:8); and Job dies "old and full of days" (42:17). In 30:2 the word can be read the same way, of men who will never reach ripe age.
review: open
