## 14:6 until, like a hired worker, he completes his day | until, like a hired worker, he enjoys his day
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb is used of paying off what is owed: the land "will pay off its sabbaths" and the people "will pay off their guilt" (Leviticus 26:34, 41, 43), and Jerusalem is told that "her soldier's service is fulfilled, her guilt is paid off" (Isaiah 40:2), where it stands beside the same word for a soldier's service that Job uses of a human life (7:1; 14:14). A hired worker owes his day, and Job has just said that a man's days are counted and that he cannot pass his limit (14:5), so "until" has its plain sense: until the day has been served out. Job has already drawn the hired worker's day as something to get through, not to enjoy: "are his days not like the days of a hired worker? Like a slave who pants for the shadow, and like a hired worker who hopes for his pay" (7:1-2); and the years of a hired worker are a term counted exactly (Isaiah 16:14; 21:16). The same verb follows "until" in 2 Chronicles 36:21, where the land lies at rest "until" it has made up its sabbaths.
other: Everywhere else in Job the verb means to be pleased with someone or to accept him: "he prays to God, and he accepts him" (33:26; also 34:9), and that is its common meaning in Scripture (Psalm 149:4; Proverbs 3:12). When Job asks God elsewhere to look away from him, it is so that he may have a little gladness before he dies: "Let him cease; let him withdraw from me, so that I may be cheerful a little, before I go" (10:20-21), which is the prayer of Psalm 39:13. Read that way, Job asks that a man be left to take what pleasure he can in his one short day, as a hired worker does in his; and the next words give the reason, that a man has no second life (14:7-12).
review: open

## 14:17 For then you would count my steps ... you would plaster over my guilt | For now you count my steps ... you plaster over my guilt
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: This call covers 14:16 and 14:17 together. The two Hebrew words that open 14:16 mean "for then" when they follow a wish, and Job has just made one: "If only you would hide me away in Sheol ... You would call, and I myself would answer you" (14:13-15). It is so after his other wishes: "If only my resentment were actually weighed ... For then it would be heavier than the sand of the seas" (6:2-3); "For then I would be silent and breathe my last" (13:19; also 3:13). "You would not keep watch on my sin" is then a plain statement, as the Hebrew stands, with no mark of a question. And the next word is "But" (14:18), which turns from the hope to what God in fact does: "you destroy the hope of a mortal" (14:19). Job wants God to know his steps: "Does he not see my ways and count all my steps?" (31:4; also 31:37), as in "You have counted my wanderings; put my tears in your bottle" (Psalm 56:8). Sin covered and put out of sight is how Scripture speaks of sin forgiven (Psalm 32:1; Isaiah 38:17; Micah 7:19).
other: The same two words are "for now" where Job speaks of how things are: "For you have now become nothing" (6:21); "For I will now lie down in the dust" (7:21). That God watches him for sin now is what Job has been saying to him all along: "if I sin, you keep watch on me" (10:14); "you write bitter things against me ... and keep watch on all my paths" (13:26-27). Guilt that is tied up and sealed is elsewhere guilt kept for the day it is punished: "The guilt of Ephraim is bound up; his sin is hidden away" (Hosea 13:12, where "bound up" is from the same word as "bag"); "Is it not stored up with me, sealed in my treasuries?" (Deuteronomy 32:34). The verb "plaster" is used elsewhere of smearing a person with lies (13:4; Psalm 119:69). Hebrew can ask a question with no mark, as at 2:9 and 11:3. The old Greek version has "nothing of my sins has escaped you".
review: open
