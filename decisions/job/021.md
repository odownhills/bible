## 21:13 in a moment | in peace
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: Everywhere else in Scripture this Hebrew word is "a moment", and so it is in this book: "test him every moment" (7:18); "In a moment they die" (34:20). Zophar has just used it of the wicked: "the joy of the godless man is for a moment" (20:5). Job turns the word around: the good of the wicked fills all their days, and the only thing that takes a moment is their dying. That is the quick death Job himself has not been given, who has "months of emptiness" and "nights of misery" (7:3), and it is how he goes on to speak of such a man: "One dies in his full strength, wholly untroubled and at ease" (21:23).
other: A verb with these letters means to be at rest: "among those nations you will find no rest" (Deuteronomy 28:65). A noun from it is "rest for your souls" (Jeremiah 6:16), and "those who are quiet in the land" (Psalm 35:20) are named from it. The old Greek version reads the word here in that way, as "rest". Then the line says how they die and not how quickly, which matches what Job says of their lives: "Their houses are at peace, without dread" (21:9). Scripture speaks so of a good death: "you will go to your fathers in peace" (Genesis 15:15). And a psalm that asks Job's question says, "I saw the peace of the wicked" (Psalm 73:3).
review: open

## 21:16 Look, their good is not in their hand | Look, is their good not in their hand?
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew has no mark of a question. As a statement it says that the good the wicked have is not theirs to make or to keep: it is in another hand. Job has said whose: "In his hand is the life of every living thing" (12:10); "The earth has been given into the hand of the wicked" (9:24); "Shall we accept the good from God and not accept the bad as well?" (2:10). Eliphaz hears the line so, for when he repeats its second half he sets God's giving in front of it: "And he himself filled their houses with good; and the counsel of the wicked is far from me" (22:18). This is what makes the words of the wicked in 21:14-15 so hard: they tell God to depart from them, and their good is from him. Scripture warns against the thought "My strength and the might of my hand have made me this wealth" (Deuteronomy 8:17). So Job adds at once that their counsel is far from him.
other: Hebrew can ask without a mark (2:9; 11:3; 14:3), and "Look" opens such a question in Exodus 8:26: "Look, if we sacrifice ... before their eyes, will they not stone us?" As a question the line sums up 21:7-13: the wicked do hold their good, to the end of their days ("They wear out their days in good", 21:13), though they have told God to depart. The old Greek version has no "not" here: "their good things were in their hands". Then "the counsel of the wicked is far from me" means that Job sees them keep their good and still does not take their side, and the next line asks how often they lose it (21:17).
review: open

## 21:17 pangs | lots
kind: two senses
call: close
why: In the plural, with nothing added, this is the word for the pangs of a woman in labor, and Scripture uses it of what God's anger brings: "throes and pangs will seize them; they will writhe like a woman giving birth", on the day of Yahweh that comes "with fury and the burning of anger" (Isaiah 13:8-9; also Jeremiah 13:21; Hosea 13:13). The writer of Job uses the word so, of mountain goats and deer giving birth (39:1-3). Eliphaz said this of the wicked man with the verb that goes with it: "All his days the wicked man writhes" (15:20). So the questions of this line take up three things the friends have said of the wicked: the lamp that is snuffed out (18:5-6), calamity (18:12), and a life of pangs.
other: The same plural is the "lines" with which land is measured out, and so the share that falls to someone: "The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places" (Psalm 16:6); "ten portions fell to Manasseh" (Joshua 17:5). It goes with the verb of this line: "your land will be portioned out with a line" (Amos 7:17). And it stands with "portion" and "inheritance", the two words Zophar has just ended on, "This is the portion of a wicked human being from God, and the inheritance decreed for him by God" (20:29): "the portion of Yahweh is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance" (Deuteronomy 32:9; also Psalm 16:5-6). Read that way Job answers Zophar's last line in its own words: how often does God really deal out such lots in his anger, on what Zophar called "the day of his anger" (20:28)?
review: open

## 21:24 His pails are full of milk | His body is full of fat
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: The Masoretic vowels make the word "milk", and so the first word, which stands only here, is something that holds it. Milk in plenty belongs with the herds Job has just given this man: "His bull breeds and does not fail; his cow calves and does not miscarry" (21:10). It also answers Zophar, who said the wicked man would not look on "the torrents of honey and curds" (20:17). In Scripture milk is the mark of a good land, "a land flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8), and Job says of his own good days, "when my steps were washed with curds" (29:6).
other: With other vowels the same letters are "fat", and the old Greek and Latin versions read it so and take the first word as a part of his body: "his inward parts are full of fat". The second half of the line is about his body, "the marrow of his bones", and so was the line of Zophar's that Job is answering: "His bones are full of his youth" (20:11). Eliphaz said of the wicked man, "he has covered his face with his fat and put on fat over his flanks" (15:27). And the psalm that asks Job's question says of the wicked, "their body is fat" (Psalm 73:4).
review: open

## 21:30 that the evil man is spared in the day of calamity, that in the day of fury they are led away | that the evil man is kept for the day of calamity, that they are led to the day of fury
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: Job calls on travelers against the friends' question, "Where is the house of the nobleman?" (21:28), so what the travelers report has to be that the evil man comes through unharmed; and the lines that follow say so: no one repays him (21:31), and he is carried to the graves with watch kept over the mound (21:32). Job has just asked how often "their calamity comes upon them" (21:17), with this same word, and has said that "the rod of God is not on them" (21:9). The verb is "hold back", and it is said of one who is kept out of harm: "He keeps back his soul from the pit" (33:18); "he did not keep back their soul from death" (Psalm 78:50). The small word before "day" can mean "in": "What will you do in the day of visitation?" (Isaiah 10:3). And to be "led" is said of people brought out safely: "in peace you will be led" (Isaiah 55:12).
other: The same verb with the same small word is "keep for" in this book: "which I have kept back for the time of distress, for the day of battle and war" (38:23); where it means "spare from", the Hebrew has the word "from" (33:18). "Led" with this small word is led to a place, and two lines later it is the same verb: "he is carried to the graves" (21:32; also 10:19); so too "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (Isaiah 53:7). Scripture says this of the wicked: "Yahweh has made everything for its purpose, even the wicked for the day of disaster" (Proverbs 16:4); "set them apart for the day of killing" (Jeremiah 12:3, after the question "Why does the way of the wicked prosper?", 12:1). The Latin version reads the line so. Read that way Job grants that a day is being kept for the evil man, and his complaint is that until it comes no one repays him to his face (21:31) and he goes to his grave in honor.
review: open
