## 9:3 he could not answer him one time in a thousand | God would not answer him one time in a thousand
kind: grammar
call: clear
why: The Hebrew says only "he would not answer him" and does not say who is who. Job goes on with the same verb and with himself as the one who cannot answer: "How much less could I myself answer him" (9:14), "I could not answer him" (9:15), "he is not a man like me, that I should answer him" (9:32); and "how much less" looks back to a line that has already said it of others. When Yahweh does speak, he says, "Let the one who argues with God answer it" (40:2), and Job replies in the words of this verse: "Once I have spoken, and I will not answer" (40:5).
other: "He" can be God: if a mortal wished to contend with him, God would not answer him once in a thousand times. Job says as much in this speech and after it: "If I called and he answered me, I would not believe that he was listening to my voice" (9:16); "I cry out to you, and you do not answer me" (30:20; also 19:7; 31:35). Elihu takes this to be Job's complaint: "Why do you contend against him? For he gives no answer for any of his words" (33:13).
review: open

## 9:13 the helpers of Rahab | the helpers of the proud
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: Rahab is named once more in this book, beside the sea and the fleeing snake: "by his understanding he wounded Rahab" (26:12-13). Isaiah and the Psalms speak of the same defeat: "Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces, who pierced the sea monster?" (Isaiah 51:9); "You crushed Rahab like one who is slain" (Psalm 89:10). Job has just said that God "treads on the heights of the sea" (9:8), and he has already asked, "Am I the sea, or a sea monster?" (7:12).
other: The name is also a word for one who is proud or overbearing: it is the word in "who does not turn to the proud" (Psalm 40:4), and Isaiah 30:7 and Psalm 87:4 use it as a name for Egypt. So the words can be read as "the helpers of the proud", any proud power and its allies, which goes with the verb: "the haughtiness of man will be bowed low" (Isaiah 2:17).
review: open

## 9:17 with a storm | for a hair
kind: two senses
call: close
why: With the vowels the Masoretic Text gives it, the word is "storm", and the main text keeps to the Masoretic vowels. It is spelled with these same letters in "Yahweh, his way is in whirlwind and in storm" (Nahum 1:3), and the verb from it stands in Job with the same spelling, of the east wind that sweeps a man from his place (27:21). A storm is how God comes to Job in the end: "Yahweh answered Job from the storm" (38:1; 40:6). The lines before are about God's strength, which shakes the earth and cannot be turned back (9:5-13).
other: With other vowels the same letters are "a hair", the word in "the hair of my flesh bristled" (4:15) and "sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judges 20:16). "For a hair" would mean for next to nothing, and it matches the second half of the line: "multiplies my wounds for nothing". Some ancient versions read it this way.
review: open

## 9:19 who will summon me | who will summon him
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Masoretic Text has "me", and in the Old Testament the main text is always the Masoretic Text. The same Hebrew word in the same form stands in Jeremiah 49:19 and 50:44: "For who is like me, and who will summon me?" As Job's own question it fits what he wants and cannot get, a hearing ("I wish to argue with God", 13:3; "Then call, and I myself will answer", 13:22), when there is no one to call the two of them into court together: "There is no arbiter between us" (9:33).
other: The old Greek version has "who then will resist his judgment?", so that the one who cannot be called to account is God, as he is the mighty one in the first half of the line. "Who will summon him" carries on from "who can turn him back? Who will say to him, 'What are you doing?'" (9:12). In Jeremiah the words are Yahweh's own, a boast that no one can summon him, and the Hebrew here can be heard the same way, as God's answer quoted: "Who will summon me?"
review: open

## 9:20 it would declare me crooked | he would declare me crooked
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb has no subject of its own, and the subject of the first half of the line is "my mouth": "my mouth would condemn me". The two halves match piece for piece ("righteous" and "blameless", "condemn" and "declare crooked"), so the plainest reading carries the same subject over. Job has just said that he could not choose his words with God (9:14), and Eliphaz throws the line back at him with both halves about Job's own speech: "Your mouth condemns you, and not I, and your lips testify against you" (15:6).
other: "He" can be God, who is the subject of almost every verb around it: he strikes, multiplies wounds, gives a fill of bitter things (9:17-18), makes an end of the blameless and the wicked (9:22). Job goes on to say it to God: "I know that you will not hold me innocent" (9:28); "then you would dip me in the pit" (9:31); and the speech continues, "Do not condemn me" (10:2). Yahweh's question to Job at the end is "Will you condemn me so that you may be righteous?" (40:8).
review: open

## 9:23 the testing of the innocent | the despair of the innocent
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: Everywhere else in Scripture this Hebrew noun is a testing: the place called Testing, "because they tested Yahweh" (Exodus 17:7), and "the great testings that your eyes saw", the blows God brought on Egypt (Deuteronomy 7:19; 29:3; also 4:34). So it can be a blow that puts people to the test, which suits the "scourge" in the first half of the line. Job has already said that God tests a mortal "every moment" (7:18), and the reader has been told that Job's own disasters are a test of whether he fears God for nothing (1:9-12; 2:3-6).
other: The same letters can be a noun from the verb "melt", which Scripture uses of courage giving way: "our heart melted" (Joshua 2:11). Job has already used a word from that verb, "one who is losing heart" (6:14). No other place in Scripture has the noun in this sense, but it fits the line simply: death comes without warning, and God mocks at the despair of people who did nothing to deserve it.
review: open

## 9:33 There is no arbiter between us | If only there were an arbiter between us
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The Masoretic Text has "not", and in the Old Testament the main text is always the Masoretic Text. It carries on from the line before: "For he is not a man like me, that I should answer him, that we should come together into court" (9:32). Job has been saying the same thing all through the speech, that no one stands over God: "who can turn him back?" (9:12).
other: The old Greek version reads a wish, "If only there were", which in Hebrew is the same letters with a different vowel; the written text of 2 Samuel 18:12 and 19:6 spells "if" with these letters. "If there were" is found elsewhere ("If only there were a sword in my hand", Numbers 22:29; also Job 16:4), while "not" joined to "there is" stands nowhere else in Scripture. The verbs that follow are in the form for a wish: "let him lay his hand on us both", "let him remove his rod from me" (9:33-34). And Job does go on to ask for someone between: "that he would argue for a man with God" (16:21).
review: open
