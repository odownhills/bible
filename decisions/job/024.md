## 24:1 Why, when times are not hidden away from the Almighty, do those who know him not gaze on his days | Why are times not stored up by the Almighty, and why do those who know him not gaze on his days
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew is "Why from the Almighty are times not hidden away, and those who know him do not gaze on his days?" "Hidden away from" is what this verb with "from" means in the other places where Scripture has them together: "For my eyes are on all their ways ... and their guilt is not hidden away from before my eyes" (Jeremiah 16:17, where it is also God from whom nothing is hidden), and Job's own "you have hidden their heart away from insight" (17:4). A "why" whose first half grants something and whose second half holds the question stands in Isaiah 5:4: "Why, when I hoped for it to yield grapes, did it yield bad grapes?" And Job has just said that God knows: "For he knows the way I take" (23:10); "many such things are in his mind" (23:14); the speech ends with "his eyes are on their ways" (24:23). So he asks why, when God knows the times, those who know him see nothing of his days.
other: "From" can also mark the one who does a thing: "the inheritance decreed for him by God" (20:29); "By Yahweh a man's steps are made firm" (Psalm 37:23). And this verb is the friends' word for what God keeps in store for the wicked: "the number of years hidden away for the ruthless" (15:20, the very same form of the verb); "God hides away his harm for his sons" (21:19). Then Job asks two things side by side, as in "Why are we counted as beasts, and why are we unclean in your eyes" (18:3): why God keeps no times of reckoning, though "there is a time for every matter" (Ecclesiastes 3:17), and why his days are never seen. "Day" and "time" stand together in this way in Ezekiel 30:3: "a day of Yahweh is near ... it will be a time of nations."

## 24:9 and they take pledges from the afflicted | and they take the infant of the afflicted as a pledge
kind: two senses
call: close
why: With the vowels of the Hebrew text the small word is "on" or "against", and the verb is the one in "you have taken pledges from your brothers for nothing, and stripped the naked of their garments" (22:6). What Scripture shows being taken from the poor in pledge is what they wear: "If you ever take your neighbor's cloak as a pledge, you must return it to him by the time the sun goes down" (Exodus 22:26); "you must not take a widow's garment as a pledge" (Deuteronomy 24:17). And the next line is "Naked they go about, without clothing" (24:10).
other: The same letters with other vowels are the word for a child at the breast: "Can a woman forget her nursing child" (Isaiah 49:15); "an infant of a few days" (Isaiah 65:20). That matches the first half of the line, "the orphan from the breast," and Scripture knows children taken for debt: "the creditor has come to take my two children for himself as slaves" (2 Kings 4:1); "we are forcing our sons and our daughters to be slaves" (Nehemiah 5:5). No old copy is known to read the word this way.

## 24:12 and God makes no charge of wrongdoing | and God does not consider the prayer, as the old Syriac version reads
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: The Hebrew text has the word of the book's first chapter: "In all this Job did not sin, and he did not charge God with wrongdoing" (1:22); and with the verb used here the writer says "his angels he charges with error" (4:18). So Job, who made no charge against God, now says that God makes none against those who do these things. That is the question the chapter opens with (24:1), and what he said before: "The earth has been given into the hand of the wicked ... If not, then who is it?" (9:24).
other: The old Syriac version reads the same letters as "prayer", and the words just before are "the soul of the wounded cries for help." Scripture sets the two together: "Even when I cry out and cry for help, he shuts out my prayer" (Lamentations 3:8). Job says the same of himself: "I cry for help, and there is no justice" (19:7); "I cry to you for help, and you do not answer me" (30:20). The verb is then the short form of "consider", as in "surely he himself would consider me" (23:6).

## 24:22 And he drags away the mighty | And he prolongs the life of the mighty
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: With a person as its object this verb is to drag or draw: "he seizes the afflicted when he drags him off in his net" (Psalm 10:9); "Do not drag me away with the wicked" (Psalm 28:3); "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fishhook?" (Job 41:1). The sentence opens with "And", which joins it to the line before: the man who feeds on the barren woman and does no good to the widow (24:21) carries off the strong as well. He is then "not sure of life", the words of Deuteronomy 28:66 ("you will not be sure of your life") and what Eliphaz said of the wicked man ("He does not believe that he will return from darkness", 15:22), and the next line answers it: "He gives him security" (24:23).
other: The verb also means to make a thing last: "Prolong your faithful love to those who know you" (Psalm 36:10); "her days will not be prolonged" (Isaiah 13:22); "you bore with them for many years" (Nehemiah 9:30, the same verb). Then God is already the one acting here, as he is in the next line, and the strength is his, as in "Is it with great strength that he would contend with me?" (23:6): he keeps the mighty alive, and such a man gets up again when he was not sure of life. That is what Job has been saying: "Why do the wicked live, grow old, and even grow mighty in wealth?" (21:7); "there is security for those who provoke God" (12:6).
review: open
