## 18:2 How long before you put an end to words? | How long will you set snares for words?
kind: two senses
call: close
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "How long will you set ... for words?", and the noun in the middle stands only here. It is read as the word for "end", in a longer spelling and in the plural. The same verb, the same noun and the same small word after it stand together once more in this book: "He puts an end to darkness" (28:3). Job has just asked the friends this very thing, "Is there an end to words of wind?" (16:3), and Bildad turns it back on him, as he opened his first speech: "How long will you utter these things, and the sayings of your mouth be a mighty wind?" (8:2). Job answers with the same "How long" and the same word for "words" (19:2). The old Greek version also takes it of stopping: "How long will you not cease?"
other: The noun is written with a letter that the word for "end" has nowhere else in Scripture (6:11; 16:3; 22:5; 28:3), so it may be a word of its own, and it is taken as "snares": "How long will you set snares for words?", that is, go hunting for them. That fits the form of the question. Elsewhere "How long will you" asks about something a person keeps on doing (8:2; 19:2), and where it asks for a thing that has not yet been done, the Hebrew says "not": "How long will you not tell the people to turn back?" (2 Samuel 2:26; also Hosea 8:5; Zechariah 1:12). Here there is no "not", and "before" has to be supplied to give the main text. The speech goes on to six words for traps (18:8-10), and Scripture speaks of snaring a person with words (Isaiah 29:21; Proverbs 6:2; Matthew 22:15). But no such noun for a snare stands anywhere else in Scripture, so this sense cannot be checked there.
review: open

## 18:3 unclean in your eyes | stupid in your eyes
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The Hebrew verb is written the way "be unclean" is written in Leviticus 11:43 ("and be made unclean by them"), a verse about creatures that are not to be eaten, and "unclean" is the word the law joins to "beast" (Leviticus 5:2; 7:21; 27:11, 27; Numbers 18:15). Job closed his speech with two thrusts at the friends: the innocent man and "the one with clean hands" stand against the godless man (17:8-9), and "I will not find a wise man among you" (17:10). Bildad's line answers both, the second with "counted as beasts" and the first with "unclean", the opposite of Job's word "clean" (17:9; the two stand together at 14:4). Being clean or pure "in the eyes" of someone is how this book talks: "I am clean in your eyes" (11:4); "the heavens are not pure in his eyes" (15:15); and Bildad himself says that the stars are not pure in God's eyes (25:5).
other: The verb can also be one that stands only here in Scripture, for being stopped up and so slow of mind. Then the second half of the line says again what the first half says, as the two halves of a line in Job often do. Beasts are Scripture's picture for those who do not understand: "I was senseless and did not know; I was a beast with you" (Psalm 73:22); "A human being in honor who does not understand is like the beasts that perish" (Psalm 49:20); God "teaches us more than the beasts of the earth" (35:11). It is the friends' sense, not their cleanness, that Job has attacked all along: "ask the beasts now, and they will teach you" (12:7); "you have hidden their heart away from insight" (17:4). And Bildad's first word of reply is "Understand" (18:2). Because that verb stands nowhere else, this sense cannot be checked in another place.
review: open

## 18:12 His vigor goes hungry | His harm is hungry
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Hebrew word is the one Bildad used five lines earlier, "his vigorous steps" (18:7), and the man's failing strength is the thread of these lines: his steps are cramped (18:7), his vigor goes hungry, his limbs are eaten (18:13). It is the word for the strength of a body: "his vigor is in the muscles of his belly" (40:16); "the beginning of my vigor" (Genesis 49:3). Hunger takes strength away: "he is hungry, and has no strength" (Isaiah 44:12).
other: With "his" attached, the word for "harm" is written with the same letters and vowels: "God hides away his harm for his sons" (21:19). Job says that a few lines after he takes up this very speech: "How often is the lamp of the wicked snuffed out, and their calamity comes upon them?" (21:17; see 18:5-6, 12). Read that way it is the harm coming to the man that is hungry. That makes a pair with the second half of the line, "calamity is ready", and leads into the next line, where something eats him (18:13). Scripture speaks so of the appetite of Sheol and of death (Isaiah 5:14; Habakkuk 2:5).
review: open

## 18:14 What he trusts in is torn from his tent | He is torn from the tent he trusts in
kind: grammar
call: close
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "is torn from his tent his trust", and "his trust" can be the thing that is torn away or a second name for the tent. As the thing torn away it stands last, after the verb and what goes with it, and that is where Bildad puts his subjects all through these lines: "seizes by the heel a trap", "holds him fast a snare" (18:9), "is scattered over his home sulfur" (18:15). The line that follows is built the same way: "lives in his tent what is not his" (18:15). The verb is the one for a cord that snaps (Judges 16:9; Ecclesiastes 4:12), and where it stands beside a tent it is what holds the tent that gives way: "My tent is destroyed, and all my cords are snapped" (Jeremiah 10:20); "none of its cords will be snapped" (Isaiah 33:20). Eliphaz said the same of those who die without wisdom: "Is not their tent cord pulled up within them?" (4:21). What a man trusts in is a thing he has, as when Job speaks of calling fine gold "my trust" (31:24), and Zophar goes on to say that what the wicked man has in his house is carried off (20:28). The old Greek version also makes the last word the thing torn out of his dwelling.
other: In Bildad's first speech the man's trust is his house itself: "whose trust is a spider's house. He leans on his house, and it does not stand" (8:14-15). So "his trust" here can be the tent. The verb is also used of people who are drawn away from a place: "they were drawn away from the city" (Joshua 8:16). A psalm tells this end of a man who trusted in the wrong thing, with a tent, a root and a trust, as here (18:14-16): God will "tear you from the tent and uproot you from the land of the living", the man who "trusted in the abundance of his riches" (Psalm 52:5, 7). And the second half of the line has the man himself taken away: "he is marched to the king of terrors".
review: open

## 18:20 Those in the west are appalled at his day, and those in the east are seized with horror | Those who come after are appalled at his day, and those who came before were seized with horror
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The two Hebrew words are "those behind" and "those in front", and each is used both of place and of time. Where Scripture sets these same two words side by side, they are west and east: "his front toward the eastern sea and his rear toward the western sea" (Joel 2:20); "half of them to the eastern sea and half of them to the western sea" (Zechariah 14:8). The lines on either side are about places: his memory perishes "from the earth", he has no name "on the face of the open country", he is chased "out of the world", no survivor is left "in the places where he stayed" (18:17-19), and "this is the place of one who does not know God" (18:21). Job is one of "the people of the east" (1:3), and he uses the same two roots for the directions in front of him and behind him (23:8). The same two words for the horror are said of peoples far off who hear of a fall: "All who live on the coasts are appalled at you, and their kings shudder with horror" (Ezekiel 27:35; also 32:10, "on the day of your downfall").
other: Said of people elsewhere, the first word is those who live later: "those who come after will not rejoice in him" (Ecclesiastes 4:16; also 1:11); "the generation to come" (Psalm 78:4; 102:18). The second is "the ancients" (1 Samuel 24:13) and "former days" (Ezekiel 38:17). A later generation that looks in horror at a place God has judged is what Moses foretells, with the sulfur of 18:15: "the generation to come ... will see the plagues of that land ... sulfur and salt" (Deuteronomy 29:22-23). Bildad rests his case on those who lived before: "ask now of an earlier generation" (8:8). The lines before are also about what lasts through time: memory, name, progeny, posterity (18:17, 19). Job uses the first word of time (19:25), and the old Greek version has "the last" and "the first" here.
review: open
