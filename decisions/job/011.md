## 11:11 and will he not look closely at it? | he sees harm without having to look closely
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew is "he sees harm and does not look closely", and Hebrew often asks a question with no word to mark it, as Job does: "Shall we accept the good from God and not accept the bad as well?" (2:10). Zophar is saying that God acts on what he sees: he "shuts someone in and calls an assembly" (11:10), and he has Job's guilt before him (11:6). A psalm has the same opening words, "for he himself knows", after a question that is marked: "Would not God search this out? For he himself knows the secrets of the heart" (Psalm 44:21). Scripture answers in the same way those who think that God sees and takes no notice: "They say, 'Yah does not see, and the God of Jacob does not discern.' ... He who formed the eye, does he not look?" (Psalm 94:7-9); "does not he who weighs hearts discern it?" (Proverbs 24:12). Job says of God, with this same verb, "I stand, and you look closely at me" (30:20).
other: Read as a plain statement, the words say that God sees harm at once and has no need to study it. Elihu says this of God: "For he does not consider a man any further, that he should go to God in judgment. He shatters the mighty without inquiry" (34:23-24). That fits the first half of the line, "he himself knows empty men": he knows already.
review: open

## 11:12 a hollow man will get sense when a wild donkey's colt is born a human being | a hollow man can get sense, though a human being is born a wild donkey's colt
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The two halves are built alike, the creature first and then what it turns into: a hollow man gets a heart, a wild donkey's colt is born a human being; and Hebrew sets a picture beside a saying with a bare "and", as in "a human being is born to misery, as sparks fly upward" (5:7). Zophar has just spoken of the "empty men" whom God knows (11:11), and he turns from them to Job with a stressed "you": "If you yourself set your heart right" (11:13). The wild donkey is the animal that is never tamed: "Who has sent the wild donkey free? ... He laughs at the noise of the city; he does not hear the shouts of the taskmaster" (39:5-7). Scripture says the same of the fool, who would buy wisdom "when he has no heart" (Proverbs 17:16): "Though you pound a fool in a mortar ... his foolishness will not leave him" (Proverbs 27:22); and it speaks of an impossible change of nature in the same way: "Can a Cushite change his skin, or a leopard its spots? Then you also can do good" (Jeremiah 13:23). In "Were you born the first human being?" (15:7) the words before "born" likewise say what one is born as.
other: In Eliphaz's saying the same two words stand together, and there "a human being" is the one who is born: "a human being is born to misery" (5:7; so also "a human being, born of woman", 14:1). Scripture speaks of people as animals without sense: "Do not be like a horse, like a mule, without understanding" (Psalm 32:9), and Ishmael is called "a wild donkey of a man" (Genesis 16:12), the same two words side by side as here. Read that way, the line says that a human being is born wild and can still be given a heart, and it leads straight into the appeal, "If you yourself set your heart right" (11:13), and the promise, "there is hope" (11:18).
review: open
