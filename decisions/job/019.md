## 19:4 And even if I have truly gone astray | And truly I have gone astray
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew has no word for "if": word for word it is "And also truly I have gone astray; with me stays my error." Hebrew can set a supposition down bare, and Job does so with this same thought: "If I have sinned, what do I do to you?" (7:20, where the Hebrew is only "I have sinned"). The next line opens with the same "truly", this time with "if" ("If you truly make yourselves great against me", 19:5), so the two lines read as two things granted for the sake of argument. Job knows of no such error: with this same verb he asked the friends, "make me understand how I have gone astray" (6:24), and he says, "I am blameless" (9:21) and "I know that I myself will be found righteous" (13:18). His point in these lines is that God, and not a fault of his own, is behind what has happened: "know then that God has wronged me" (19:6).
other: The same two opening words stand together once more in this book, and there they affirm: "Truly, God does not act wickedly" (34:12). Read that way Job owns an error and says that it is his own affair and not the friends'. The verb is the one for a wrong done without knowing it (Leviticus 4:13; Numbers 15:22), and a man uses it when he confesses: "I have been a fool and have gone very far astray" (1 Samuel 26:21). Job does own to faults: "the guilty deeds of my youth" (13:26), "my rebellion" and "my guilt" (7:21). The old Greek version also reads the line as an admission.
review: open

## 19:17 and I am repulsive to the sons of my mother's womb | and I plead with the sons of my mother's womb
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The first half of the line says what Job's breath is to his wife, and the second half is built the same way: what Job is to his brothers. The lines on either side say how each group treats him: his servant does not answer (19:16), young children "reject" him, the men of his inner circle "detest" him (19:18-19). The letters are those of the verb "show favor", but in this simple form that verb always means being kind to someone, never asking for kindness: "I will show favor to whom I will show favor" (Exodus 33:19); "Have pity on me" (19:21); "let there be no one to show favor to his orphans" (Psalm 109:12). "I show favor to my brothers" does not belong in a list of those who have turned from him. So the word is taken as another verb written the same way, which stands only here. Its sense is drawn from this line and cannot be checked anywhere else in Scripture.
other: These are the letters of the verb for favor and pleading, and Job uses that verb in the line before and again four lines later: "with my mouth I plead with him" (19:16); "Have pity on me, have pity on me" (19:21). The form is letter for letter the one in Exodus 33:19. The old Greek and Latin versions both took it so, of Job entreating. Read that way the line carries on from the servant who will not answer: Job has to plead with his servant, and with his own brothers too. Against it, "plead" is everywhere else another form of the verb (8:5; 9:15; 19:16).
review: open

## 19:25 and at the last he will rise on the dust | and the Last will rise on the dust
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew word is "later" or "last", and it is used of time to come: "a later day" (Isaiah 30:8; Proverbs 31:25), "a later generation" (Psalm 78:4; 102:18). Two of those verses are, like this one, about words written down so that they will still speak afterward: "write it on a tablet with them and inscribe it in a book, that it may be for a later day, forever" (Isaiah 30:8, with the "inscribe", the "book" and the "forever" of 19:23-24); "Let this be written for a later generation" (Psalm 102:18). The next line opens with the word it is formed from, "And after" (19:26), so Job is setting out what comes later, one thing after another. He expects to be gone first: "a few years will come, and I will go the path from which I will not return" (16:22).
other: Where Hebrew says "afterward" or "in the end" with this word, it puts "in" or "to" before it (Deuteronomy 13:9; 2 Samuel 2:26; Numbers 2:31). Here the word stands bare, as it does when Yahweh says it of himself: "Thus says Yahweh, the King of Israel and his redeemer, Yahweh of hosts: I am the first and I am the last" (Isaiah 44:6; also 48:12). "Redeemer" and "the last" stand together there as they do here. The two halves of a line in Job answer each other, and read this way "the Last" answers "my redeemer" as "will rise" answers "lives". Job goes on at once to say that he will gaze on God (19:26).
review: open

## 19:26 from my flesh I will gaze on God | without my flesh I will gaze on God
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew is "from my flesh", and with a verb of seeing "from" names the place a person looks out from: "from the top of the rocks I see him, and from the hills I catch sight of him" (Numbers 23:9); "gazing from the windows" (Song of Songs 2:9); "a mortal looks from far off" (36:25). What follows is the sight of bodily eyes: "my eyes will see" (19:27). In the lines just before, his flesh is the wasted body he still has: "My bone clings to my skin and to my flesh" (19:20); "do not have your fill of my flesh" (19:22, the same Hebrew form as here). Job has said that the dead do not come back: "one who goes down to Sheol does not come up" (7:9); "a man lies down and does not rise" (14:12). And this is how the book ends, with Job still alive: "now my eye has seen you" (42:5).
other: "From" can also mean "away from", and so "without", and it does twice in this book: "you will lift your face without blemish" (11:15); "their houses are at peace, without dread" (21:9). The first half of the line has his skin struck off, and skin and flesh are what he was clothed with (10:11). "On the dust" (19:25) is where the dead lie in Job (17:16; 20:11; 21:26), and Job expects to die before he is cleared: "I will go the path from which I will not return" (16:22); "graves are mine" (17:1). That is why he wants his words cut into the rock (19:23-24). He has wished for a meeting with God beyond death: "If only you would hide me away in Sheol ... You would call, and I myself would answer you" (14:13-15). And a psalm speaks so: "I will gaze on your face ... when I awake" (Psalm 17:15).
review: open

## 19:27 and not a stranger | and not as a stranger
kind: grammar
call: clear
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "and my eyes have seen, and not a stranger". Set after "my eyes", the last words name who will not be the one to see: someone else. Scripture sets "a stranger" against a person's own self in just this way: "Let a stranger praise you, and not your own mouth" (Proverbs 27:2); "no stranger shares its joy" (Proverbs 14:10). The line presses the one point three times over: "I myself", "for myself", "my eyes". And the book ends on it, with his own sight set against what others had told him: "By the hearing of the ear I had heard of you, and now my eye has seen you" (42:5). The old Greek version has "and not another".
other: The words can also say how Job will see God, or how God will then be to him: "not as a stranger". All through this speech Job has been made a stranger. Those who know him "have become nothing but strangers" (19:13); his household "count me as a stranger; I have become a foreigner in their eyes" (19:15); and God himself "counts me as one of his foes" (19:11; also 13:24). Read that way the line turns all of that around: with his own eyes he will see God, and no longer as one who is shut out.
review: open

## 19:29 for wrath brings the punishments of the sword | for wrath is guilt that the sword punishes
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew is three words with no verb, "wrath, guilty deeds of the sword", and the middle word means both a guilty deed and what is paid for it: "My punishment is too great to bear" (Genesis 4:13); "no punishment will come on you for this" (1 Samuel 28:10). A line with no verb can say what wrath leads to: "The wrath of a king is messengers of death" (Proverbs 16:14). The words that follow give the aim, "so that you may know that there is a judgment", and that is said of punishments that fall. Wrath and the sword go together as God's: "my anger will burn, and I will kill you with the sword" (Exodus 22:24). Job uses this word of God's wrath: "let him drink of the wrath of the Almighty" (21:20). And this is how it ends for the three: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends" (42:7). The old Greek version also has wrath coming on wrongdoers.
other: Everywhere else in this book the plural of the middle word is "guilty deeds" (13:23, 26; 22:5), and Job twice names a wrong by who punishes it, in the same pattern as here: "that is a guilt for the judges" (31:11; so also 31:28). Read that way the wrath is the friends' own, the fury with which they "pursue me as God does" (19:22, 28), and Job calls it a crime for the sword. They have just said that the root of the matter is found in him (19:28); he answers that the guilt lies in their pursuit of him.
review: open
