## 22:4 Is it for your fear of God that he corrects you | Is it out of fear of you that he corrects you
kind: grammar
call: clear
why: The Hebrew is "your fear", which can be the fear Job has or the fear someone has of Job. Eliphaz has used this very word of Job twice, and both times it is Job's fear of God: "Is not your fear of God your confidence" (4:6); "You yourself even do away with fear of God" (15:4). The next line gives the answer he is asking for: it is not for that, but "Is not your evil great" (22:5). It is what he said in his first speech, that God does not strike a person for doing right: "who, being innocent, has perished?" (4:7).
other: The lines before say that God gets nothing from a man (22:2-3), and "nor is he afraid of him" would carry that on: God has no need to take Job to court to protect himself. The same Hebrew word with "your" is the fear that others have of a person in Deuteronomy 2:25: "This day I will begin to put the dread of you and the fear of you on the peoples."
review: open

## 22:17 what the Almighty could do for them | what the Almighty could do to them
kind: two senses
call: close
why: Eliphaz is quoting what Job said of the wicked: "And they say to God, 'Depart from us; we do not wish for the knowledge of your ways. What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? And what do we gain if we appeal to him?'" (21:14-15). Their question there is what good he is to them. And Eliphaz answers it in his next words: "And he himself filled their houses with good" (22:18). The Hebrew verb with this small word is said of what God does for people: "which you have done for those who take refuge in you" (Psalm 31:19); "all our works you have done for us" (Isaiah 26:12).
other: With the same verb and the same small word Job asked God, "If I have sinned, what do I do to you" (7:20). Then these men were daring God to punish them, as others do in Scripture: "Let him hurry, let him speed his work, so that we may see it" (Isaiah 5:19). That fits what is said of them just before, that they "were seized before their time" (22:16). The old Greek version takes it so: "what will the Almighty bring on us?"
review: open

## 22:29 When people are brought low and you say, 'Rise up,' he will save the one with downcast eyes | For they are brought low, and you will say, 'It was pride'; but he saves the one with downcast eyes
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The lines on either side are promises about what Job's word will do once he has returned to the Almighty: "You will plead with him, and he will hear you" (22:27); "You will decree a thing, and it will stand for you" (22:28); and one who is not innocent "will be delivered by the cleanness of your palms" (22:30). So the word Job says here is a word for those who are brought low, and God acts on it. The Hebrew word comes from the verb "rise", which this book uses of a head that is lifted (10:16) and of a plant that grows tall (8:11). And the book ends this way: Yahweh tells Eliphaz, "my servant Job will pray for you, for I will lift his face" (42:8).
other: Everywhere else in Scripture this word is "pride": "and to hide pride from a man" (33:17); "my soul will weep in secret because of pride" (Jeremiah 13:17). And Scripture sets pride beside "bring low", the verb of this line: "A human being's pride will bring him low" (Proverbs 29:23); "look on everyone who is proud and bring him low" (40:11); "those who walk in pride he is able to bring low" (Daniel 4:37). The second half of the line is then the other side of one saying, as in Psalm 18:27: "you save an afflicted people, and eyes that are lifted high you bring low." Read that way Eliphaz is not promising Job a word that helps others; he is giving the rule that Job is to learn from his own fall.
review: open

## 22:30 one who is not innocent | the innocent man, as the old Greek and Latin versions read
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: The Hebrew text has a small word before "innocent" that means "not". It is the first part of the name Ichabod, "no glory", which is explained there: "The glory has gone into exile from Israel" (1 Samuel 4:21). The second half of the line needs it: this person is delivered by the cleanness of someone else's palms, Job's, and an innocent man would not need that. The line before speaks of the same kind of person, one who is brought low and has downcast eyes (22:29). And it is how the book ends, with Eliphaz himself as the one who is not innocent: "My anger burns against you and your two friends ... my servant Job will pray for you" (42:7-8). Scripture knows of the guilty spared for the sake of the righteous: "If I find in Sodom fifty righteous people inside the city, I will forgive the whole place because of them" (Genesis 18:26).
other: Outside that name the small word stands nowhere else in Scripture as "not". The old Greek and Latin versions have no "not" here ("He will deliver the innocent"), and the Greek goes on, "and be delivered by your clean hands". That is what Eliphaz has taught from the start: "who, being innocent, has perished?" (4:7). In this speech the innocent man is the one who watches the wicked fall (22:19). And Scripture says of Job by name that such a man delivers only himself: "even if these three men were in it, Noah, Daniel and Job, they by their righteousness would deliver their own lives" (Ezekiel 14:14).
review: open
