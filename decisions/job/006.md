## 6:10 leap for joy | recoil, or steel myself
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew verb stands nowhere else in Scripture, so the sentence has to decide. It is set beside "I would still have my comfort", and the reason Job gives for both is a glad one: "for I have not concealed the words of the Holy One." Its form is the one for what a speaker wants to do, as in "let me speak in the bitterness of my soul" (10:1), which suits gladness better than flinching. The ancient Greek version has "leap", and leaping is how Scripture pictures joy: "you will go out and leap like calves from the stall" (Malachi 4:2); "rejoice in that day and leap for joy" (Luke 6:23).
other: In later Hebrew the same word is used of drawing back from something that burns, which gives "I would recoil in anguish": Job's comfort would hold even while he flinched under pain that does not spare. A related word in a sister language means "be hard", which gives "I would steel myself in anguish", and that matches his resolve elsewhere: "I hold fast to my righteousness and will not let it go" (27:6).
review: open

## 6:14 even if he abandons the fear of the Almighty | or that friend abandons the fear of the Almighty
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew says only "and the fear of the Almighty he abandons" and does not say who "he" is. The sentence is about "one who is losing heart", and the friend comes in only as "his friend", so the plainest reading carries on with the same man: he is owed loyalty even if, in his despair, he lets go of the fear of the Almighty. That is the question the book opened with, whether Job will go on fearing God when everything is taken from him (1:9-11; 2:3-5), and in this speech Job asks that a broken man's words not be held against him: "my words have been rash" (6:3), "the sayings of a despairing man" (6:26). "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for trouble" (Proverbs 17:17).
other: "He" can be the friend: the friend who holds back loyalty is the one who abandons the fear of the Almighty. Scripture often ties kindness to the weak to fearing God: "You shall not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God" (Leviticus 19:14; so also 25:17, 36, 43; Nehemiah 5:9, 15), and Job says dread of God kept him from wronging the poor (31:16-23). It leads straight into the next line, "My brothers have been treacherous" (6:15), and Eliphaz may be throwing it back at 15:4: "But you yourself do away with the fear of God." Several ancient versions read the whole verse this way: "whoever withholds loyalty from his friend abandons the fear of the Almighty."
review: open

## 6:18 The paths of their way wind about | Caravans turn aside from their way
kind: two senses
call: close
why: With the vowels the Masoretic Text gives it, the first word is "the paths of", joined to "their way", and the main text keeps to the Masoretic vowels: the torrents of 6:15-17 are still the subject, and their courses wander off into the waste and are lost. The order of the lines favors it: the caravans come in at 6:19, where they look, hope, arrive and are put to shame (6:19-20), and that would come too late if they had already perished in 6:18. "Go up" is said of water that vanishes: "the layer of dew went up" (Exodus 16:14).
other: With other vowels the same letters are "caravans", the word for the caravan of Ishmaelites in Genesis 37:25 and the caravans of Dedanites in Isaiah 21:13, and in the very next line these letters are the "caravans of Tema" (6:19). Then it is the travelers who leave their road to look for the torrent, go up into the waste and die there: "he makes them wander in a waste with no way" (12:24; Psalm 107:40).
review: open

## 6:21 you have become nothing | you have become like it
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The rules follow the written Hebrew text wherever it can be read, and here it can: the written word is "not", so the friends "have become a 'not'", nothing at all. It fits the picture Job has just drawn: the torrents "are brought to an end" and "perish" (6:17-18), and the caravans that trusted them came and found nothing (6:20). Job says the same of his friends later: "worthless healers, all of you" (13:4).
other: The traditional way of reading the verse aloud has a word that sounds the same and differs by one letter, "to it" in place of "not": "you have become like it", that is, like the torrent that fails those who trust it. That is the comparison Job began at 6:15: "My brothers have been treacherous like a torrent."
review: open

## 6:26 and count the sayings of a despairing man as wind | when the sayings of a despairing man are for the wind
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The second half of the line has no verb of its own. The verb of the first half is the one Job uses again and again, with the same small word that stands before "wind" here, to mean "count as": "you count me as your enemy" (13:24; 33:10), "they count me as a stranger" (19:15), "he counts iron as straw" (41:27). So it most naturally carries over. The friends do treat his words this way: Bildad begins, "How long will ... the sayings of your mouth be a mighty wind?" (8:2), and Eliphaz speaks of "knowledge of wind" (15:2); Job throws it back: "Is there an end to words of wind?" (16:3).
other: The second half can be a sentence of its own, with nothing carried over: "the sayings of a despairing man are for the wind", that is, the wind carries them off and they should not be held against him. Job has just said, "that is why my words have been rash" (6:3), and it would give the reason for his question: why correct words that are gone with the wind?
review: open
