## 20:10 His sons will appease the poor | The poor will crush his sons
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: The Masoretic vowels make the verb a form of "be pleased with" that means to make someone pleased, so the sons have to win over the poor. A neighboring form of the same verb is used of a servant winning back his master: "With what could this man make himself pleasing to his master?" (1 Samuel 29:4). The second half of the line is about making good what was taken, "his hands will give back his riches", and Zophar says it again: "He gives back what he toiled for" (20:18). The law says this of one who has robbed, "he shall give back what he took by robbery" (Leviticus 6:4), and "seized" in 20:19 is that word for robbery. So both halves of the line say that the poor are made good.
other: The same letters can be read as the verb "crush", with "the poor" as its subject, and the old Greek version reads them so: lesser men destroy his sons. The Latin version also has the sons crushed. "Crush" is the verb Zophar uses a few lines on, with the same word for "the poor": "he has crushed, he has abandoned the poor" (20:19). Read that way, what the man did to the poor is done to his sons, as Scripture says: "As you have done, it will be done to you" (Obadiah 15). Eliphaz said of the fool that "his sons ... are crushed in the gate" (5:4, another word for crushing), and Job takes the friends to mean that God stores up a man's harm "for his sons" (21:19).
review: open

## 20:19 he has seized a house, and he will not build it up | he has seized a house that he did not build
kind: grammar
call: close
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "a house he has seized, and he does not build it." The verbs for what the man did are in the form for finished acts ("crushed", "abandoned", "seized"). "Build" is in the other form, the one Zophar uses all through these lines for what the man will not get to do: "will not swallow it", "will have no enjoyment" (20:18), "will not escape" (20:20), "will not endure" (20:21). The clause is joined on with "and", like "and will not swallow it" in the line before, and the old Greek version also joins it on: "he seized a dwelling and did not set it up." Scripture speaks this way of a house a man gets no good of: "You will build a house, and you will not live in it" (Deuteronomy 28:30); "you have built houses of cut stone, and you will not live in them" (Amos 5:11, said to those who trample on the poor). To "build" a house is also to make a household stand and grow (Ruth 4:11; Proverbs 24:3).
other: Hebrew poetry can leave out "that", and it can use this verb form for the past ("the day on which I was born", 3:3). So the words can also mean "a house that he did not build": he took for himself what another man's work had made. Scripture describes in this way what a person has without having worked for it: "cities that you did not build, and houses full of every good thing that you did not fill" (Deuteronomy 6:10-11; also Joshua 24:13), though there the verb is in the form for finished acts. Read that way the whole of 20:19 is a list of his wrongs, and what comes of them waits for 20:21: "that is why his good will not endure."
review: open

## 20:20 he will not escape with what he desires | he lets nothing that he desires escape
kind: grammar
call: close
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "with what he desires he does not deliver", and the verb has no object. The same form stands with no object where a man fails to get away: "the swift of foot will not escape" (Amos 2:15, between two lines that say "will not deliver his life"). The old Greek version reads it so here: "he will not be saved." "What he desires" is his treasure, as in "you make what he treasures melt away like a moth" (Psalm 39:11), and Scripture says that treasure does not get a man out: "Neither their silver nor their gold will be able to rescue them in the day of the wrath of Yahweh" (Zephaniah 1:18; also Proverbs 11:4). The lines on either side are built the same way, first what he did and then what he will not have: "he has seized a house, and he will not build it up" (20:19); "There is no survivor from his eating; that is why his good will not endure" (20:21). And the speech ends with all that he has stored going from him (20:26, 28).
other: Everywhere else in Job this form of the verb has an object and means to deliver someone, to let him get away (6:23; 22:30; 29:12). So "what he desires" can be its object: of all that he desires he lets nothing escape. Then both halves of the line describe his greed, "he has known no ease in his belly" and this, and so does the half-line that follows: "There is no survivor from his eating" (20:21). Read that way 20:19-21 is one long charge that opens with "For" and is closed by "that is why his good will not endure."
review: open

## 20:22 the hand of everyone in misery will come upon him | the whole power of misery will come upon him
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: The Masoretic vowels make the word "one in misery", a person, as in "Why is light given to one in misery?" (3:20). So the words are "every hand of one in misery": all who suffer turn their hands on him. Scripture speaks so of a man whom everyone is against: "his hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him" (Genesis 16:12). In this speech the ones he made suffer are the poor, "he has crushed, he has abandoned the poor" (20:19), and it is the poor whom his sons have to appease (20:10).
other: With other vowels the same letters are the noun "misery", and the old Greek and Latin versions read it so: "every distress will come upon him." In this book "the hand of" a thing is its power: "from the power of the sword" (5:20); "into the power of their rebellion" (8:4). "Misery" is the friends' word for what the wicked bring on themselves: "those who plow harm and sow misery harvest it" (4:8); "They conceive misery and give birth to harm" (15:35); and in Zophar's first speech, "you yourself will forget misery" (11:16). The same verb is used of what the wicked man fears: "What the wicked dreads will come upon him" (Proverbs 10:24). Read that way the second half of the line says what the first half says, "he will be in distress", and no people are in it.
review: open

## 20:23 and rain it on him as his food | and rain it on him, into his flesh
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The Hebrew word stands only here and in Zephaniah 1:17, and it is made from the verb "eat", the verb of "they eat the bread of wickedness" (Proverbs 4:17) and of the usual word for bread. The line itself is about filling his belly, and from 20:12 on the speech has been about what the man eats: evil sweet in his mouth, "his bread turns in his stomach" (20:14), wealth swallowed and vomited up (20:15), "his eating" (20:21). When God rains something on people in Scripture it is often their food: "I am going to rain bread from heaven for you" (Exodus 16:4); "he rained manna on them to eat ... he rained flesh on them like dust" (Psalm 78:24, 27). That psalm goes on, "their food was still in their mouths, and the anger of God rose against them" (78:30-31), and it has the very words of this line: "he sent on them the burning of his anger" (78:49). Another psalm makes what is rained on the wicked their serving: "He will rain snares on the wicked; fire and sulfur and a scorching wind will be the portion of their cup" (Psalm 11:6), as Zophar ends with "the portion of a wicked human being" (20:29). And Job answers in these terms: "let him drink of the wrath of the Almighty" (21:20).
other: In the one other place where the word stands it is part of the body: "their blood will be poured out like dust, and their flesh like dung" (Zephaniah 1:17). That passage shares more with this speech: "dung" (20:7), treasure that cannot rescue (Zephaniah 1:18; 20:20), and "the day of the wrath of Yahweh" beside "the day of his anger" (20:28). Zophar's word for bread in 20:14 is the usual one, so this other word may be meant for another thing. And the lines that follow are about the man's body: a bow pierces him, and the point comes out of his back and from his gall (20:24-25). Read that way God's anger rains down into the man's flesh, as Job said of himself: "the arrows of the Almighty are in me" (6:4).
review: open
