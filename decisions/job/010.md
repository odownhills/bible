## 10:11 you wove me together | you hedged me in
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The lines around it are a run of pictures from a maker's work: clay (10:9), milk curdled into cheese (10:10), a body clothed with skin and flesh (10:11). Bones and sinews lie inside the skin and flesh and tie the body together, as in Ezekiel's valley, where sinews are laid on the bones, then flesh, then skin over them (Ezekiel 37:6-8); they do not cover anything. The one other place in Scripture that uses this verb of a child being formed has the same setting, "in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13), and goes on with the word for a weaver's patterned work: "I was embroidered in the depths of the earth" (Psalm 139:15; the word of Exodus 26:36).
other: Everywhere else in Job a verb with these letters means to hedge in, shut in or cover: "around whom God has put a hedge" (3:23), "who shut in the sea with doors" (38:8), "the lotus trees cover him with their shade" (40:22); and so in the rest of Scripture: "with his feathers he will cover you" (Psalm 91:4; also Exodus 40:21; Psalm 140:7). "Clothed" and "covered" make a natural pair, and the next line is about protection: "your care kept watch over my spirit" (10:12). Read that way, God fenced him about with bones and sinews, the picture the adversary used of God's care for Job (1:10).
review: open

## 10:15 and seeing my affliction | and see my affliction
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew word can be a describing word, "seeing", or a command, "see". As a describing word it is the pair of the one before it, "full of disgrace and seeing my affliction", and the sentence runs on without a break into "And if my head rises" (10:16). To see affliction is to live through it: "I am the man who has seen affliction" (Lamentations 3:1). Job speaks the same way of seeing good: "my eye will not see good again" (7:7); "they do not see good" (9:25).
other: As a command it is a cry to God, and these very words are a prayer elsewhere: "See my affliction and my misery" (Psalm 25:18); "See my affliction" (Psalm 119:153; also Psalm 9:13; Lamentations 1:9); and it is God who sees it: "God saw my affliction and the toil of my hands" (Genesis 31:42). Job breaks into such cries all through what he says to God here: "Do not condemn me" (10:2), "Remember now" (10:9). And the describing word "seeing" is found nowhere else in Scripture, while the command is common.
review: open

## 10:17 relays of troops are against me | turn after turn of a soldier's service is mine
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: The Hebrew is three words, "changes and an army with me", and the word for "army" is also the word for the term a soldier serves. The lines just before it are all things God does against Job: "you hunt me", "you work wonders against me", "You renew your witnesses against me and increase your resentment toward me" (10:16-17). An army coming on in fresh relays carries that on, and "relays" answers "you renew". Job pictures God's attack as an army elsewhere: "the terrors of God are lined up against me" (6:4); "he runs at me like a warrior" (16:14); "His troops come together; they build up their way against me and camp around my tent" (19:12). The word for "changes" is used of men sent out in relays (1 Kings 5:14).
other: In Job's mouth these two words stand together once more, and there they are his own hard service and the relief that ends it: "All the days of my soldier's service I would wait, until my relief came" (14:14). He has already said, "Is there not a soldier's service for a mortal on earth?" (7:1). Read that way the line is about Job's lot and not about God's troops: one term of hard service after another. "With me" is how Job speaks of what someone has in him or with him (9:35; 10:13; 23:14).
review: open

## 10:20 Let him cease; let him withdraw from me | Cease, and withdraw from me
kind: copies differ
call: clear
why: The rules follow the written Hebrew text wherever it can be read, and here it can: the two verbs are written as "let him cease", "let him withdraw", said about God and not to him. Job turned from "you" to "he" in the same way at the end of the chapter before: "then you would dip me in the pit" (9:31), and then "Let him remove his rod from me, and let the terror of him not terrify me" (9:34). So the words Job says he will say to God (10:2) are closed at 10:19, and 10:20-22 are spoken about him.
other: The traditional way of reading the verse aloud has commands, "Cease, and withdraw from me", so that Job goes on speaking to God to the end of the chapter. He has said this to God before: "Leave me alone, for my days are a breath" (7:16); "How long will you not look away from me?" (7:19). The same prayer in the Psalms is a command, with the words that follow here: "Look away from me, so that I may be cheerful, before I go and am no more" (Psalm 39:13).
review: open
