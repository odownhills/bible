## 27:3 for as long as my breath is in me | for my breath is still whole in me
kind: grammar
call: close
why: After "As God lives" Hebrew goes on to the thing sworn with the small word that opens this line, and the "if" of the oath can come after it, as it does here in 27:4: "As Yahweh the God of Israel lives, who has kept me from doing you harm, if you had not hurried and come to meet me, not one male would have been left to Nabal by the light of morning" (1 Samuel 25:34). So the line is part of the oath and says how long it holds, which is what Job says again in the next lines: "until I breathe my last I will not remove my blamelessness from me" (27:5). The word "still" is used in this way for the length of a life: "I will sing praise to my God while I still am" (Psalm 104:33; 146:2); "the God who has shepherded me all my life until this day" (Genesis 48:15).
other: The same Hebrew words, with "my life" for "my breath", stand in one other place, and there they are a statement and not a length of time: "anguish has seized me, for my life is still whole in me" (2 Samuel 1:9). Read that way the line stands apart from the oath: Job says that he is alive and in possession of himself, with the breath God gave him still in him ("the breath of the Almighty gives me life", 33:4), and then swears that his lips do not speak injustice.
review: open

## 27:8 when he is cut off | though he makes his profit
kind: two senses
call: ask
why: Job used this verb of his own death, and there too it stood beside "hope", with God as the one who cuts: "If only ... God would give what I hope for, and God would be willing to crush me, would let loose his hand and cut me off!" (6:8-9). It is the weaver's word for cutting the cloth from the loom, "he cuts me off from the loom" (Isaiah 38:12), and Job has already joined hope to the weaver's work: "My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and they come to an end without hope" (7:6). The form of the verb here is the simple one, and it is used of cutting in "cut them off on the heads of them all" (Amos 9:1). Read so, the two halves of the line say one thing, and God, who is named in the second half, is the one who acts in both. Bildad said, "the hope of the godless man perishes" (8:13).
other: In this simple form the verb most often means to make gain by greed or violence, and Scripture sets that gain against a man's life: "So are the paths of everyone who is greedy for gain; it takes away the life of those who have it" (Proverbs 1:19); also Proverbs 15:27; Jeremiah 6:13; Habakkuk 2:9. Its noun is "profit" in 22:3. Read that way Job says that all the godless man has gained is no hope to him when God takes his life, and the lines that follow are about his gains: "If he heaps up silver like dust ... the innocent man will portion out the silver" (27:16-17). Zophar said, "He has swallowed wealth, and he will vomit it up" (20:15). On this reading the verb needs no unnamed subject.
review: open

## 27:15 will be buried by the plague | will be buried in death
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Hebrew word is "death", and Scripture uses it for deadly sickness where it stands beside the sword and hunger: "Those who are for death, to death; and those who are for the sword, to the sword; and those who are for famine, to famine" (Jeremiah 15:2); "let their men be killed by death, their young men struck down by the sword in battle" (Jeremiah 18:21); also Jeremiah 43:11. Here the sword and the lack of bread stand in the line before (27:14), and this is the third of the three: those whom the sword and hunger leave are taken by the plague.
other: The plain sense of the word is "death", and the words can be taken as they stand: his survivors are buried in death, with death itself for their burial and no one to mourn them, as the second half says in the words of Psalm 78:64, "his widows did not weep." Jeremiah speaks of such deaths: "They will die of deadly diseases; they will not be mourned or buried" (Jeremiah 16:4).
review: open

## 27:19 and will not be gathered | and will not do so again
kind: copies differ
call: close
why: With the vowels of the Hebrew text the verb is "be gathered". Scripture says it of the dead who are taken up and buried: "they will not be gathered or buried; they will be dung on the face of the ground" (Jeremiah 8:2; also 25:33); "you will not be gathered or collected" (Ezekiel 29:5); "he was gathered to his people" (Genesis 25:8). "Lie down" is said of dying: "Together they lie down on the dust" (Job 21:26). So the rich man lies down and no one gathers him in, which goes with what has just been said of his household: "His survivors will be buried by the plague, and his widows will not weep" (27:15).
other: With other vowels the same letters are the verb "do again", which is spelled in this way in "You must not go on giving the people straw" (Exodus 5:7) and in 1 Samuel 18:29. The old Greek and Syriac versions read it so here. Zophar said this of the wicked man: "An eye has glimpsed him and will not do so again" (20:9). Read that way he lies down rich for the last time, and the second half of the line says the same thing over: "he opens his eyes, and he is no more." Job has put lying down and being no more together before: "For I will now lie down in the dust; you will search for me, and I will be no more" (7:21).
review: open

## 27:22 And it lets fly at him and does not spare; from its hand he flees headlong | And God lets fly at him and does not spare; from his hand he flees headlong
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The two verbs just before have the east wind as their subject, "The east wind lifts him up ... and it sweeps him from his place" (27:21), and this line is joined to them with "and" and names no one new. In Hebrew a thing can have a "hand", which is its power: "from the power of the sword" (5:20, where the Hebrew is "the hands of the sword"); "Death and life are in the hand of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21). The east wind is what breaks and carries off in Scripture: "the east wind has broken you in the heart of the seas" (Ezekiel 27:26); "With the east wind you break the ships of Tarshish" (Psalm 48:7).
other: The Hebrew says only "he", and God is often "he" with no name in Job (16:9-14). Job has just said, "I will teach you about the hand of God" (27:11), and that this portion is "from the Almighty" (27:13), so "from his hand he flees" can be that hand. "Does not spare" has been said of God twice: "though he does not spare" (6:10); "he splits open my kidneys and does not spare" (16:13). In Zophar's picture of the same end the one who strikes is God, with no name: "he will send the burning of his anger on him and rain it on him" (20:23). And this verb is used of God throwing in a storm: "Yahweh threw great stones on them from the sky" (Joshua 10:11); "He throws his ice like crumbs" (Psalm 147:17). Read that way the next line can be about God too: "He claps his hands at him."
review: open

## 27:23 It claps its hands at him and hisses at him from his place | People clap their hands at him and hiss at him from his place
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew verbs are singular and no subject is named, as in the lines just before, where the verbs follow the east wind (27:21-22). So the line is read as the last thing the storm does: it claps and hisses over him as it drives him "from his place", the words that close 27:21. The word for "hiss" is also the word for whistling (Isaiah 5:26; Judges 5:16), and Scripture lets the things God has made clap their hands: "all the trees of the field will clap their hands" (Isaiah 55:12); "Let the rivers clap their hands" (Psalm 98:8).
other: To clap the hands and hiss at someone is what people do at a ruin, and Scripture says it in these same words: "All who pass along the way clap their hands at you; they hiss and shake their head at the daughter of Jerusalem" (Lamentations 2:15); "everyone who passes by her hisses and shakes his hand" (Zephaniah 2:15, with singular verbs, as here); also 1 Kings 9:8; Jeremiah 49:17; Ezekiel 27:36. Hebrew can use a singular verb with no subject for people in general, and the ending on "hands" here is one that most often means "their". Elihu uses the verb of a man's scorn: "he claps among us" (34:37). The friends' pictures of the wicked man end with what other people make of him (18:20; 20:7). If God is the one who strikes in 27:22, the line can be read of him: "I too will strike palm against palm" (Ezekiel 21:17).
review: open
