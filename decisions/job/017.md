## 17:5 One who informs on friends for a share—the eyes of his sons will fail | A man invites friends to a share, while the eyes of his sons fail
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: Word for word the Hebrew is "for a share he tells friends, and the eyes of his sons fail". With a person as its object the verb "tell" is used of informing on him, and it is friends who do it: "'Denounce him! Let us denounce him!' say all my close friends" (Jeremiah 20:10). Job has just asked who will clasp his hand in pledge (17:3); a friend who gives a man up for gain is the opposite of one who stands as his guarantor, and Job has charged the three with this before: "You would even cast lots over an orphan, and bargain over your friend" (6:27). A "share" is what falls to each when something is divided, spoil among it (Genesis 14:24; 1 Samuel 30:24). Failing eyes are a judgment in the friends' own mouths, "the eyes of the wicked will fail" (11:20), and so is harm that reaches a man's sons, "His sons are far from safety" (5:4); Job turns both back on them.
other: Everywhere else in Job where this verb has a person as its object it is simply "tell": "To whom have you told words?" (26:4); "I would tell him the number of my steps" (31:37). A "share" is also a portion of food (Deuteronomy 18:8; Habakkuk 1:16), and eyes fail from hunger: "their eyes fail because there are no plants" (Jeremiah 14:6; compare Lamentations 2:11; 4:17). Read that way the line is a picture of the three, following on from "you have hidden their heart away from insight" (17:4): a man who calls friends to share what he has while his sons go without, as they hand out a wisdom they do not have ("I will not find a wise man among you", 17:10). A third reading takes the first word as "smooth talk", a sense it has in Proverbs 7:21, which gives "one who speaks flattery to friends" (compare Proverbs 29:5).
review: open

## 17:6 and I am one whose face is spat on | and formerly I was like a drum
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The Hebrew noun stands only here as a common word. The first half of the line is about open contempt, "a byword among the peoples", and the two halves of a line in Job answer each other. Job sets the same two things side by side again: he has become "a byword" to those who laugh at him, and they do not keep their spit from his face (30:9-10). Spitting in the face is how Scripture marks a person as disgraced (Numbers 12:14; Deuteronomy 25:9; Isaiah 50:6). The word that follows the noun is made from the word for "face", and it is used of direction, not of time, in Jeremiah 7:24 ("backward and not forward"). And the verb is the form Job uses of what he is now, in a line built the same way: "I am one who is a laughingstock to his friend" (12:4).
other: The word that follows the noun is "formerly" in almost every other place where it stands, in Job too: "all who had known him formerly" (42:11; also Deuteronomy 2:10; Ruth 4:7; Psalm 102:25). And the noun is one letter longer than the word for a hand drum, which is played where people are glad (21:12; Genesis 31:27; Exodus 15:20). Read that way, Job sets what he once was beside what he has been made, as he does at length in 29:2-25 and 30:1 ("But now they laugh at me"). Written as it is here, the noun is elsewhere only the name Topheth, the place in the Valley of Hinnom that was made a horror (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31-32; 19:6, 11-13); "a Topheth before them" would say much the same as the main text.
review: open

## 17:16 It will go down to the bars of Sheol, when there is rest together on the dust | Will it go down to the bars of Sheol? Shall we go down together to the dust?
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The last word of the verse is the noun "rest" (Ecclesiastes 4:6; Isaiah 30:15), which is said of one who never saw the sun: "this one has more rest than that one" (Ecclesiastes 6:5). Rest, and rest together, is how Job has spoken of the grave from his first speech: "then there would be rest for me" (3:13); "there those whose strength is worn out are at rest. Prisoners are untroubled together" (3:17-18). "On the dust" is where the dead lie, and what a man had lies there with him: "it will lie down with him on the dust" (20:11); "Together they lie down on the dust, and maggots cover them" (21:26). The verse has no mark of a question, and so it answers the two questions of 17:15: his hope goes down to Sheol with him.
other: The same word is "coming down" in Isaiah 30:30 ("the coming down of his arm"), from a verb "go down" that takes "on" (Psalm 38:2, "your hand has come down on me"; also Jeremiah 21:13). With other vowels the letters are "we go down", and the old Greek version reads the verse as two questions, with "shall we go down together" in the second. That gives the second half of the line the same act as the first half, the way the two halves of a line in Job answer each other. The verse would then carry on the questions of 17:15: Hebrew can ask without a mark (2:9; 11:3; 14:3), and the small word before "together" opens a question at 6:12 ("Is my strength the strength of stones, or is my flesh bronze?").
review: open
