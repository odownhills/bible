## 12:5 there is contempt for misfortune; it is ready for those whose foot slips | A despised torch is the man whose foot is ready to slip
kind: two senses
call: close
why: The first Hebrew word can be read as the one word "torch" or as "for misfortune"; the letters and the vowels are the same. Job uses this word for misfortune twice more, once of this very thing, how one man looks on another's fall: "If I rejoiced at the misfortune of one who hated me" (31:29; also 30:24; Proverbs 24:22). Read this way the two halves of the line are built alike, "for misfortune" and "for those whose foot slips", and the second half is like what the friends say of the wicked man: "a day of darkness is ready at his hand" (15:23); "calamity is ready" for him (18:12). The line carries on from "a laughingstock" (12:4), and a psalm puts the same two words together: "the mockery of the untroubled, the contempt of the proud" (Psalm 123:4).
other: "Torch" is a word this book uses ("Out of his mouth go torches", 41:19), and Scripture speaks of a person's life as a light that can go out: "the light of the wicked is snuffed out ... and his lamp above him is snuffed out" (18:5-6; also 21:17). In a psalm it is the man himself who is "ready" to fall, in the line after "when my foot slips": "For I am ready to stumble" (Psalm 38:16-17). Read that way the line says that a man about to fall is, to one who is untroubled, a torch to be despised.
review: open

## 12:6 for the one who brings a god in his hand | for the one into whose hand God brings it
kind: grammar
call: ask
why: The Hebrew is "for the one who brought God in his hand", and "God" can be the thing brought or the one who brings. To bring something "in the hand" is the ordinary way of saying that a person carries it: "they brought him the offering that was in their hand" (Genesis 43:26). The line then names the same people three ways: destroyers, those who provoke God, and the man whose god is what his own hand holds. Scripture says this of the raider, with the same word for "god": "guilty, he whose strength is his god" (Habakkuk 1:11; also "he sacrifices to his net", 1:16), and of the violent: "it is in the power of their hand" (Micah 2:1). Job answers with "But": even the beasts know "that the hand of Yahweh has done this", and that every life is in his hand (12:7-10). That is what such people deny: "Our hand is high, and it is not Yahweh who has done all this" (Deuteronomy 32:27).
other: With "God" as the one who brings, the words are "for the one into whose hand God has brought it". The subject then stands in its usual place after the verb, and the clause is built like the one four lines later: "in whose hand is the life of every living thing" (12:10). This is what Job says elsewhere: "The earth has been given into the hand of the wicked" (9:24); God shines "on the counsel of the wicked" (10:3); and it is where he is going here: "the hand of Yahweh has done this" (12:9). Jeremiah says the same to God about the wicked: "You have planted them, and they have taken root" (Jeremiah 12:2).
review: open

## 12:12 Among the aged is wisdom, and in length of days understanding | Is wisdom among the aged, and understanding in length of days?
kind: grammar
call: close
why: The Hebrew has no word that marks a question, and as it stands it is a statement. It is what the friends have told Job: "ask now of an earlier generation ... Will they themselves not teach you?" (8:8-10), and Job claims no less for himself: "I too have sense, as you do" (12:3); "What you know, I myself also know" (13:2). The next line is built the same way and goes beyond it: "Among the aged is wisdom ... With him are wisdom and might" (12:12-13). Job does not say that elders have no discernment; he says that God takes it away (12:20). Elihu knows it as the accepted saying: "I said, 'Days should speak, and many years should make wisdom known'" (32:7).
other: Hebrew often asks a question with no word to mark it, as Job's wife does: "Are you still holding fast to your blamelessness?" (2:9); and the line before this one is a question: "Does not the ear test words?" (12:11). Job began by mocking the friends' claim to wisdom, "with you wisdom will die" (12:2), and he has just sent them to the beasts, not to the fathers (12:7-8). Eliphaz answers as if Job had denied it: "Both the gray-haired and the aged are among us, older than your father" (15:10). Elihu says it outright: "It is not the great who are wise, nor the elders who understand justice" (32:9).
review: open

## 12:18 the discipline of kings | the bonds of kings
kind: two senses
call: close
why: With the vowels the Masoretic Text gives it, the word is "discipline", the word in "do not reject the discipline of the Almighty" (5:17), and the main text keeps to the Masoretic vowels. The discipline of kings is the hard rule they lay on their people: "My father disciplined you with whips, and I will discipline you with scorpions" (1 Kings 12:11). God undoes it and puts the kings themselves in a captive's loincloth, as the lines on either side lead counselors and priests away stripped (12:17, 19).
other: With other vowels the same letters are the word for bonds, and that is the word this verb goes with elsewhere: "Who has undone the bonds of the wild donkey?" (39:5); "You have undone my bonds" (Psalm 116:16; also Isaiah 52:2). It makes a pair with the second half of the line, where a loincloth is bound on, and Isaiah uses this verb of kings and their waists: "I will undo the waists of kings", that is, take off their belts (Isaiah 45:1). Read this way it is the kings' own belt that God takes off before he binds a loincloth on them.
review: open

## 12:23 and leads them away | and guides them
kind: two senses
call: clear
why: The Hebrew verb is "lead", and the sentence has to say where. The first half of the line sets a raising beside a ruin: "He makes nations grow and destroys them", and the second half is built the same way. Every line from 12:14 on is something God undoes, and the next lines go on to chiefs made to wander in a waste (12:24-25). The same verb in the same form tells of a nation carried off: "the king of Assyria took Israel into exile to Assyria and led them to Halah" (2 Kings 18:11).
other: Most often this verb is a kind leading, and it is said of God and the nations: "you guide the peoples on earth" (Psalm 67:4); "Yahweh alone guided him" (Deuteronomy 32:12); "he guides me in paths of righteousness" (Psalm 23:3). Job uses it so of himself: "from my mother's womb I guided her" (31:18). Read that way the line says that God spreads nations abroad and then leads them on their way, as "he wounds, and his hands heal" (5:18).
review: open
